Self-represented (pro se) filing

Filing a writ of mandamus yourself

The steps for filing a writ of mandamus without a lawyer, in order, checked against the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. They start from a widely shared 2024 community walkthrough, with its errors corrected. Your district's local rules override anything here.

Pay for one consultation first. An immigration attorney who belongs to the American Immigration Lawyers Association (AILA) can tell you three things. Will your delay likely count as unreasonable in your district? Does anything in your file make forcing a decision risky? Are your complaint and service correct? Find one through AILA Lawyer Search. A writ compels a decision, not an approval. This page is not legal advice.

One fast result does not predict yours The 2024 poster won approval three business days after service, on unusually strong facts: an interviewed, complete application pending two years past a one-month expectation. While your case is inside the posted USCIS processing estimate, the government can argue you are asking to jump the queue. That is the first of the TRAC factors courts weigh.
You are responsible for every citation you sign When you sign a complaint, Rule 11(b) certifies that its legal contentions are warranted by existing law, and courts hold self-represented filers to that. Courts have sanctioned pro se filers for citing cases that do not say what the filing claims. The 2024 template, drafted with an AI chatbot, mis-describes all three of its cases. Read every case you cite, or cite none: a delay complaint can rest on the statutes and your facts.

The steps, corrected

Most district courts publish a pro se guide, and many have a free help desk or clinic. Use yours alongside these steps.

1. Before you file

  • Consult an AILA attorney about your specific facts, timing and district.
  • Document every earlier step: USCIS case inquiry, congressional inquiry replies, and a demand letter with its certified-mail receipt. See the remedies guide for each step.
  • Screenshot the current USCIS processing time for your form and office on the day you file.
  • If you pay anyone to draft or edit, check them against their state bar's public license lookup. Some districts require you to disclose that a lawyer drafted your filing.
  • If your case has already been refused at an embassy, including a 221(g) refusal, read the 221(g) warning first. Courts have treated a refusal as a final decision, so there is usually nothing left to compel.

2. Choose the court

Under 28 U.S.C. § 1391(e)(1), a suit against federal officials can be filed where a defendant resides, where a substantial part of the events happened, or where the plaintiff resides. Most people file in the district where they live. If the plaintiff lives abroad, the residence option is usually not available, and filers commonly use the District of Columbia, where the federal defendants reside. Ask an attorney which district's case law is better for your facts.

3. Name the right plaintiff

The plaintiff is the person who filed the stuck form. For an I-130 that is the petitioner; the beneficiary is often added as a co-plaintiff. For an I-485 it is the applicant. If an I-130 and I-485 are pending together, both people are usually plaintiffs.

4. Name the right defendants

Sue officials in their official capacity, using the current officeholders: check each agency's leadership page on the day you file, not a template. If an official leaves while the case is pending, Rule 25(d) substitutes the successor automatically.

  • Case pending with USCIS: USCIS, the USCIS Director and the Secretary of Homeland Security. Complaints commonly also name the Attorney General.
  • Case at NVC or an embassy: the Department of State, the Secretary of State, and the relevant embassy or consular official. See Embassy and NVC cases.

Whoever you name, you must also serve the US Attorney for your district and the Attorney General (step 8).

5. Draft the complaint

  • Replace every red instruction in the template, including pronouns, form type, receipt numbers, field office and dates.
  • If you start from the 2024 template, delete its Saleh v. Ridge paragraph: that court found a five-year delay not unreasonable and ruled for the government. Han Cao v. Upchurch does not hold that exceeding processing times is enough. Al Karim v. Holder was decided on 29 March 2010 on an eight-year delay, under the APA. Read any case before you cite it.
  • Describe 8 U.S.C. § 1571(b) as supporting context. It states a 180-day goal, and courts are split on whether it creates any enforceable duty.
  • Ask for costs, such as the filing fee, rather than attorney's fees: a self-represented plaintiff has none to recover.
  • State your facts precisely: filing date, months pending, every contact with USCIS and your representatives, and concrete harm (for example family separation, an expiring work permit, or medical needs).
  • End with a declaration under 28 U.S.C. § 1746 ("I declare under penalty of perjury that the foregoing is true and correct. Executed on [date]."), then sign and date it.
  • Black out personal data as Rule 5.2(a) requires. Show only the last four digits of Social Security and account numbers, only the year of birth, and only initials for minors. This applies to exhibits too, such as passport pages.

In immigration benefits cases, Rule 5.2(c) limits remote electronic access to the parties and their attorneys. The public can still see the docket and read filings at the courthouse, so redact anyway.

6. Assemble exhibits and copies

Put a cover page ("Exhibit A", "Exhibit B" and so on) in front of each piece of evidence: receipt notice, processing-time screenshot, inquiry confirmations, congressional replies, demand letter and its mailing receipt. Count your copies: one for the court, one for each named defendant, one each for the US Attorney and the Attorney General if they are not already named, and one for yourself.

7. File with the clerk

  • Civil cover sheet (JS 44): one. Tick U.S. Government Defendant as the basis of jurisdiction, nature of suit 465 Other Immigration Actions, and Original Proceeding as the origin. Download it from US Courts or your court's website.
  • Summons (AO 440): one for each defendant, with names and titles exactly as in the complaint. Ask the clerk whether they also want summonses addressed to the US Attorney and the Attorney General. Download it from the US Courts civil forms page.
  • Filing fee: $405. If you cannot pay, file an application to proceed without prepaying fees (AO 240) instead.
  • Ask whether your court accepts pro se filing by email or electronic upload. Many do now, and the rules vary.

8. Serve the government, correctly

Under Rule 4(m) you have 90 days from filing to complete service. Each copy is the complaint, exhibits and the stamped summons.

  • Have an adult who is not a plaintiff do the mailing. Rule 4(c)(2) bars a party from serving, and courts differ on whether a plaintiff's own certified mailing counts.
  • Send by Certified Mail with return receipt (or registered mail), the methods Rule 4(i) names, not Priority Mail Express. Send to:
    • the civil-process clerk at the US Attorney's office for your district;
    • the Attorney General in Washington, DC;
    • each named agency and official.
  • Take addresses from each agency's current official service-of-process page, for example the USCIS Service of Process Notice, and from your US Attorney's office website. Do not copy them from a 2024 template.
  • Also email a courtesy copy to USCIS if its service notice still invites that. The email does not replace the certified mail.
  • Keep every tracking number and return receipt.

9. File proof of service

Once everything is delivered, file the proof of service. The standard way is page 2 of each AO 440 summons ("Proof of Service"), completed and signed by the person who mailed it, with the tracking printouts and return receipts attached. The date the US Attorney's copy was delivered starts the government's 60 days.

10. After service

  • An Assistant US Attorney will usually contact you. They may ask for more time, propose putting the case on hold while USCIS acts, or propose an agreed date for a decision. Get any agreement in writing and filed with the court.
  • Meet every court deadline, and keep your mailing address current with the clerk. Missing a deadline can get your case dismissed.
  • If the government moves to dismiss, you will have a deadline to respond in writing. This is the point where many self-represented filers hire an attorney.

11. When USCIS decides

Once USCIS approves the case, the suit is moot. File a notice of voluntary dismissal under Rule 41(a)(1)(A)(i) if the government has not answered, or a stipulation of dismissal if it has. The court's system notifies the government's attorney once they have appeared, so you usually do not mail it. Wait for the actual approval notice, not just an interview notice or a status change. If USCIS denies the case, the mandamus suit is also over, because the court compelled a decision, not an approval. Talk to an attorney about your options at that point.

Risks and costs

The remedies guide covers these in full. In short: filing early risks dismissal under the TRAC factors and costs you $405. Forcing a decision can bring out a problem that leads to a denial. And if you lose, the court can order you to pay the government's ordinary court costs. An AILA consultation is the cheapest way to find out which of these apply to you.

Step 1: Check if you can apply

Reviewed 11 Nov 2026

You are here: You have a green card and want to know whether you can apply for US citizenship yet.

What happens: You need enough years as a permanent resident, enough days physically in the US, no long breaks abroad, and 3 months in your current state. You can file up to 90 days before you reach the full residence period.

Do this first: Use the filing date calculator to find your earliest date, then check the rules below.

Action list

  1. Find your earliest filing date with the filing date calculator.
  2. List every trip outside the US since your green card's "Resident since" date, with dates out and back. Check them against your CBP I-94 travel history.
  3. Add up your days abroad, and check that you were in the US for at least half of the 3- or 5-year period.
  4. Check that no single trip lasted more than 6 months. If one did, read continuous residence before you file.
  5. Make sure you have lived in your current state or USCIS district for at least 3 months on the day you file.

Tools for this step

  • Filing date calculator: your earliest filing date by family visa category, with the forms, fees and documents that apply
  • N-400 preparation list: what to gather before you file, saved in your browser

The 5-year rule: most green card holders

You can apply when all of these are true:

  • You are 18 or older when you file.
  • You have been a permanent resident for at least 5 years.
  • You have lived in the US continuously for those 5 years (see continuous residence).
  • You were physically in the US for at least 30 months (913 days) of those 5 years.
  • You have lived for at least 3 months in the state or USCIS district where you file.

You must also show good moral character, an attachment to the principles of the Constitution, and pass the English and civics tests. Those are covered in steps 2 and 5. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part D, Ch. 1]

The 3-year rule: married to a US citizen

If you are married to and living with a US citizen, you can apply after 3 years instead of 5. All of these must be true on the day you file:

  • You have been a permanent resident for at least 3 years, and have lived in the US continuously for those 3 years (see continuous residence).
  • You have lived in marital union with your spouse for the whole 3 years before filing, and your spouse has been a US citizen for all of that time.
  • You were physically in the US for at least 18 months (548 days) of those 3 years.
  • You have lived for at least 3 months in the state or USCIS district where you file.

The 3 years of marriage, and of your spouse's citizenship, must be complete on the day you file. After you file you must stay married, and your spouse must stay a US citizen, until you take the oath. The 90-day early filing window does not shorten them. If you came on a CR1 visa, file your I-751 to remove conditions first; see the calculator for how the two forms fit together. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part G, Ch. 3]

Continuous residence: long trips abroad

Continuous residence means the US stayed your main home for the whole 3- or 5-year period, and it must continue until you take the oath. Short trips are fine. Long ones are not:

A single trip of…What it means
6 months or lessNo break. Many trips can still raise questions if the US no longer looks like your main home.
More than 6 months, less than 1 yearUSCIS presumes a break. You can overcome it with evidence that you kept your US job, your close family stayed in the US, and you kept your US home.
1 year or moreAn automatic break, unless USCIS approved Form N-470 before you left (only for certain kinds of work abroad). With the 5-year rule you can file again 4 years and 6 months after you return. You can file from 4 years and 1 day, but you must then overcome the presumption of a break. With the 3-year rule the waits are 2 years and 6 months, or 2 years and 1 day with the same condition.

Claiming to be a "nonresident" on your US tax return, or not filing because you thought you were one, can also suggest you gave up your permanent residence. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part D, Ch. 3]

Physical presence: counting days

Physical presence is a count of days. You must have been in the US for at least half of the period: 913 days for the 5-year rule, or 548 days for the 3-year rule. The days you leave and return both count as days in the US.

Holding a green card for the whole period is not enough on its own. Keep your passports and travel records, because the N-400 asks for every trip. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part D, Ch. 4]

3 months in your state or district

You file with the USCIS office covering where you live, and you must have lived there for at least 3 months before you file. If you file early under the 90-day rule, the 3 months are counted before your interview instead. If you move to a different state or district, the 3 months start again. A trip abroad of less than a year does not change your state, as long as you come back to the same home. Students can file where they study, or at their parents' home if their parents support them. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part D, Ch. 6]

Filing up to 90 days early

You can file up to 90 days before you reach the 3 or 5 years of permanent residence. USCIS counts back 90 days from the day before you would first meet the residence requirement. You are not eligible to become a citizen until the full period has passed, so the interview and oath come after it.

File even one day too early and USCIS can reject or deny your application. The calculator works out the date for you. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part D, Ch. 6]

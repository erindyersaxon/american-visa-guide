Step 7: Your oath ceremony

Reviewed November 11, 2026

You are here: Your N-400 has been approved, and you are waiting for your oath ceremony, or you have your ceremony notice.

What happens: You go to a public ceremony, hand back your green card, and take the Oath of Allegiance. You become a US citizen at the moment you take the oath, and you leave with your Certificate of Naturalization.

Do this first: Check the date and place on your ceremony notice (Form N-445), and keep your record clean until the day.

Action list

  1. Check your ceremony notice (Form N-445) for the date, time and place. If you cannot go, ask to reschedule right away.
  2. Answer the questions on the notice about what has happened since your interview. Answer as of the ceremony day.
  3. Bring your notice and your green card. You will hand the card back.
  4. Take the oath, then check your certificate for mistakes before you leave.
  5. Keep your certificate somewhere safe, and apply for your US passport.

USCIS or court ceremony: it depends on where you live

There are two kinds of ceremony. Which one you get depends on your area, and it can change how long you wait after your interview.

  • USCIS (administrative) ceremony: held by your field office. Some offices hold ceremonies often, and some hold them on the same day as an approved interview. If yours does, you may become a citizen the day of your interview. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part J, Ch. 4]
  • Court (judicial) ceremony: held by a federal or state court. In some areas the court has exclusive authority to give the oath, so everyone there goes to a court ceremony. These can be less frequent, which can mean a longer wait. You can also choose a court ceremony in some areas. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part J, Ch. 6]

Your ceremony notice tells you which kind you have, and where. If you asked to change your name on your N-400, that is usually done at a court ceremony, because a court must approve a name change. The court gives you a document showing the change.

Asking for an earlier ceremony

You can ask USCIS, or the court, for an expedited ceremony if you have a compelling reason, such as a serious illness in your family, a disability that makes a public ceremony hard, or urgent travel or work. Explain the reason in writing, with evidence. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part J, Ch. 6]

Between approval and the ceremony

You are still a permanent resident until you take the oath. Approval is not the end of the process:

  • A second officer reviews every approved case before the ceremony.
  • If USCIS learns something that could make you ineligible, such as a new arrest, it will not schedule your ceremony. It will send you a notice, and you have 15 days to respond. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part J, Ch. 4]
  • Your good moral character and continuous residence must last until the oath. Avoid any arrest or citation, keep filing and paying taxes, and avoid long trips abroad.
  • Travel on your green card and your current passport, as before.

Your ceremony notice: Form N-445

Form N-445, Notice of Naturalization Oath Ceremony, gives your date, time and place. If your field office holds a ceremony on the day of your interview, you may take the oath that day instead. [USCIS: Naturalization ceremonies]

The back of the notice has a questionnaire. The questions cover only the time since your interview. Since your interview, have you:

  1. married, or been widowed, separated or divorced?
  2. traveled outside the United States?
  3. committed any crime or offense you have not been arrested for?
  4. been arrested, cited, charged, indicted, convicted, fined or imprisoned for breaking any law, including traffic violations?
  5. joined or become connected with any organization?
  6. deserted from, claimed exemption from, or been discharged from military service?
  7. changed your willingness to bear arms for the US, do noncombatant service, or do work of national importance if the law requires it?
  8. been involved in polygamy, illegal gambling, prostitution, smuggling people into the US, drug trafficking, lying to get immigration benefits, or habitual drunkenness?

How to fill it in: answer on the day of your ceremony, before you go, in black ink. Then add the date, the city and state where you filled it in, your signature and your current address. The date you write should be your ceremony date. Even a speeding ticket since your interview is a "yes". If someone read the form to you in another language, they must fill in and sign the interpreter section.

If you answer "yes", bring documents about it. For example, bring your marriage certificate or divorce decree, travel documents such as your passport, your arrest records and court dispositions, or your military discharge papers (DD-214). An officer reviews your answers at check-in before you can take the oath. [USCIS: Form N-445] [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part J, Ch. 5]

What to bring

  • Your ceremony notice (Form N-445), with the questionnaire filled in and signed.
  • Every green card you have, valid or expired. You must hand them back at check-in. The only exceptions are if you showed at your interview that your card was lost and you tried to recover it, or if you never had permanent residence because you qualified through military service.
  • All reentry permits and refugee travel documents, valid or expired, and any other documents USCIS issued you, such as work permits (employment authorization cards). These are also collected.
  • Photo ID, such as a driver's license or passport.
  • Documents about anything you answered "yes" to on the N-445.

USCIS asks you to dress in a way that respects the occasion. The ceremony is on federal property, so check the items you cannot bring. Many ceremonies allow guests, but rules on guests, phones and cameras vary by place, so read any extra instructions USCIS sends you. [USCIS: Naturalization ceremonies]

If you need an accommodation because of a disability, such as a sign language interpreter, ask as soon as you can at uscis.gov/accommodations or by calling the USCIS Contact Center at 800-375-5283 (TTY 800-767-1833). [Form N-445]

On the day

  1. Check-in. An officer reviews your N-445 answers and collects your green card.
  2. The ceremony. It usually includes speeches, the national anthem, the Pledge of Allegiance and a short USCIS video, such as "Faces of America".
  3. The Oath of Allegiance. Everyone recites the oath together, in English. You become a US citizen at this moment.
  4. Your certificate. You receive your Certificate of Naturalization (Form N-550).

The oath is in English even if you did not have to take the English test. You may bring an interpreter to help you understand it. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part J, Ch. 2]

Changes to the oath

  • "On oath" and "so help me God": you can ask to leave these words out, or to affirm instead of swear. You do not need to give a reason.
  • Bearing arms or noncombatant service: you can ask to leave out these clauses if religious training and belief, or a deeply held moral or ethical code, prevents you from promising them. You must show this at your interview.
  • Disability: USCIS can waive the oath for someone who cannot understand it because of a physical or developmental disability or mental impairment. A guardian or close family member acts for them. Ask before the ceremony, usually with Form N-648 or a written medical evaluation.

[Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part J, Ch. 3]

Your Certificate of Naturalization

Your certificate is your proof of citizenship until you get a US passport.

  • Check it before you leave the building: your name, date of birth, country of birth and other details. Tell USCIS about any mistake before you leave the ceremony. If you find it later, or lose the certificate, you will need Form N-565, which has a fee unless the mistake was USCIS's. [USCIS: Naturalization ceremonies]
  • Keep the original safe. You need it to apply for your passport.
  • Do not laminate it. Passport agencies accept only original, unaltered documents, and lamination counts as altering it. In 2020, a new citizen in Denver had her passport application refused because she had laminated her certificate, and a replacement cost $555. [Denver7, Sept. 23, 2020]
  • Do not copy it casually. The certificate warns that copying it without lawful authority is illegal. Making a copy for an official application, such as a passport, is allowed.

If you cannot go

Send your N-445 back right away to the USCIS office handling your case, with a letter asking for a new date and explaining why you cannot attend. Use the USCIS office finder to check which office that is. USCIS will then send you a new ceremony date. If you are in the military, you can also call the USCIS Military Help Line at 877-247-4645. [USCIS: Naturalization ceremonies]

Missing two ceremonies

If you miss two or more ceremonies without good cause, USCIS treats you as having abandoned your application. It will send you a notice, and you have 15 days to show good cause. If you do not, USCIS can deny your application, even though it was approved. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part J, Ch. 4]

Right after the ceremony

  • US passport: your Citizenship Welcome Packet includes a passport application. Apply as soon as you can. You send your original certificate with the application, and it is returned. Leave plenty of time before any trip abroad: as a citizen, you need a US passport to leave and come back.
  • Voting: you get a voter registration application at the ceremony, after you take the oath. You can also register at a post office, motor vehicle office or your county elections office. Register only after you have taken the oath.
  • Social Security: wait at least 10 days after your ceremony, then update your record to show you are a citizen. Use Form SS-5, by mail or at a Social Security office. [USCIS: Naturalization ceremonies]
  • Family: your children under 18 with green cards who live with you may become citizens automatically. See children in the US. Petitions you filed for relatives as a green card holder may also move to a faster category.

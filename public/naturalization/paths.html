Which path to citizenship are you on?

Reviewed November 11, 2026

You are here: You want to become a US citizen, but you are not sure which rules apply to you.

What happens: Most people with a green card apply on Form N-400 after 5 years. Some can apply sooner, and some children are already citizens and do not need to apply at all.

Do this first: Find the first row in the table below that matches you. Then read that section.

You can file up to 90 days before you reach 3 or 5 years. The filing date calculator works out your date.

The 5-year rule: most green card holders

This is the standard path. It applies to you if you got your green card through a parent, a brother or sister, an adult child, a spouse who had a green card (F2A), a parent who had a green card (F2B), a job, or the diversity visa lottery. It also applies if you were married to a US citizen but are now divorced, widowed, or separated, or your spouse has not been a citizen for 3 years.

You need 5 years as a permanent resident, at least 30 months of that time physically in the US, and 3 months in your state or USCIS district. Step 1 explains each rule. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part D, Ch. 1]

Refugees and asylees

Refugees and asylees use the 5-year rule, but the clock starts earlier than the day the green card was approved. A refugee counts as a permanent resident from the date they arrived in the US. An asylee counts as a permanent resident from 1 year before USCIS approved their green card. The "Resident since" date on your card should already show this earlier date. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part D, Ch. 2]

The 3-year rule: married to a US citizen

If you are married to and living with a US citizen, you can apply after 3 years instead of 5. On the day you file, all of these must be true:

  • You have been a permanent resident for at least 3 years.
  • You have been married to and living with your spouse for the whole 3 years.
  • Your spouse has been a US citizen for the whole 3 years.

You must stay married until you take the oath. If you divorce, separate, or your spouse dies before then, you will need to use the 5-year rule instead. If your spouse was a green card holder when they sponsored you and became a citizen later, the 3 years start from the day they became a citizen. Step 1 has the full rules. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part G, Ch. 3]

Conditional residents: the 2-year green card

If you came on a CR1 visa, or got a green card less than 2 years into your marriage, your first green card lasts 2 years. Your time on it counts toward the 3 or 5 years, so you do not start again when you get your 10-year card.

USCIS must approve your Form I-751 (petition to remove conditions) before it can approve your N-400. You can file the N-400 while the I-751 is still pending. If you do, USCIS will decide the I-751 before or at the same time as your N-400, sometimes at the same interview. Bring evidence of your marriage, such as joint bank statements, a lease, or children's birth certificates, in case the officer asks about the I-751. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part G, Ch. 5] [Vol. 6, Part I, Ch. 3]

If you never file the I-751, or USCIS denies it, you lose your green card and can be placed in removal proceedings. Do not skip it.

Abused spouses and children of US citizens (VAWA)

If you got your green card because your US citizen spouse or parent abused you or treated you with extreme cruelty, you can apply after 3 years as a permanent resident. You do not need to still be married to or living with that person. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part G, Ch. 3]

Federal law keeps VAWA cases confidential. Talk to an accredited representative or attorney before you file.

Spouses of US citizens working abroad

If your US citizen spouse works abroad for the US government (including the military), certain US companies, or another qualifying employer for at least 1 year, you may be able to apply with no minimum time as a permanent resident or in the US. You must plan to live abroad with your spouse and come back to the US when the job ends. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part G, Ch. 4]

The surviving spouse of a US citizen who died while serving on active duty in the US military can also apply with no minimum time. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part G, Ch. 1]

Military service

  • One year of honorable service in peacetime: you need a green card, but not 3 or 5 years with it. File while you are serving or within 6 months of leaving.
  • Any honorable service during a designated period of hostilities: you may not need a green card at all.

Current and former service members pay no N-400 fee. Your unit or base legal office can help. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part I]

Children in the US: you may already be a citizen

A child becomes a US citizen automatically, without applying, on the day all of these are true before they turn 18:

  • At least one parent is a US citizen, by birth or naturalization.
  • The child has a green card.
  • The child lives in the US in the legal and physical custody of the US citizen parent.

This means that when a parent naturalizes, their children under 18 with green cards who live with them often become citizens on the same day. These children should not file an N-400. To get proof of citizenship, apply for a US passport or file Form N-600. Stepchildren do not qualify unless the citizen parent has adopted them. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part H, Ch. 4]

Children living outside the US

A child under 18 who lives abroad with a US citizen parent can apply for citizenship on Form N-600K without a green card. The citizen parent, or that parent's own US citizen parent, must have lived in the US for at least 5 years, including 2 years after turning 14. The child must travel to the US for the interview and oath. If the child was born abroad to a US citizen, they may have been a citizen from birth. See the CRBA guide. [Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part H, Ch. 5]

Not sure, or more than one row fits?

Pick the path that lets you apply soonest, but only if every condition is true on the day you file. If you have been arrested, spent more than 6 months abroad in one trip, or have problems with your taxes, read step 2 and talk to a free legal service before you file.

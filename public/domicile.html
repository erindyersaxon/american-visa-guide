The petitioner's US domicile

A joint sponsor can fix income. Nobody can fix the petitioner's domicile. If the petitioner cannot show a US domicile, the case is refused, whatever a joint sponsor earns. This page tells you where you stand and what evidence carries weight.

A joint sponsor cannot cure it “If a petitioner cannot satisfy the domicile requirement, the petitioner fails to qualify as a ‘sponsor’ for the purposes of submitting Form I-864. A joint sponsor cannot be accepted and the applicant must be refused pursuant to INA 212(a)(4).” (9 FAM 601.14-7(a)(3)(c))

A case can survive low petitioner income: a joint sponsor files alongside, and the case goes on. A petitioner without a US domicile is a different problem, because the petitioner is the sponsor and no second filer can supply it. It catches couples living in the UK more than most, because living together in the UK while the case runs is the natural choice.

Where you stand

Domicile means your principal residence is in the United States and you intend to keep it there. You need both halves. Find your position below.

Your situationWhere you stand
You live in the US Nothing to solve. Carry the ordinary evidence: US tax filings, utility bills, state ID.
You are abroad for a set period and kept your US home Show three things: you left for a limited, and not indefinite, period; you meant to keep your US domicile when you went; and real ties continue. The guidance names students, contract workers and NGO volunteers as model cases. “Limited, not indefinite” is the rule that catches couples. Moving abroad to be with your partner, with no end date, is not a temporary absence, whatever US accounts you keep.
You are abroad on qualifying US employment The guidance already treats you as US-domiciled. See the box below: some petitioners qualify without knowing it.
You are abroad and your US domicile has lapsed You must re-establish it. The officer must be satisfied that you have taken real steps and live in the US, or will by the date the person you sponsor immigrates. You may travel together. You may not follow later.
Check this before you assume you have a problem A US citizen living abroad for a set period is domiciled in the US if they work for one of these (9 FAM 601.14-7(b), INA 319(b)(1)):
  • the US government
  • a recognised US research institution
  • a US firm or corporation engaged in whole or in part in developing US foreign trade or commerce, or a subsidiary of one
  • a public international organisation the US joins by treaty or statute
  • a US religious denomination, as authorised clergy or a missionary
The third item reaches further than petitioners expect. An LPR sponsor abroad also counts as US-domiciled if they obtained the preservation of residence benefit under INA 316(b) or 317.

What counts as evidence

The Department lists what establishes domicile: opening a US bank account, moving money to the US, making US investments, looking for US work, registering children in US schools, applying for a Social Security number, and voting in local, State or Federal elections. Property, a mortgage and a lease are missing from that list.

A lease proves less than you think The green sheet lists property and leases, so include yours. But a lease or mortgage shows an address, not a residence: a landlord does not know whether you are in the country. Do not let it stand alone. The evidence that counts comes from living there:
  • US tax filings showing domestic earned income
  • a W-2, or a US job contract or signed offer with a start date
  • utility bills in your name across several months
  • school or university enrollment
The officer asks whether you have “in fact taken up principal residence” in the United States.

If you are starting from nothing, two points help. Your US address can be a house, an apartment, or a stay with family or friends. You need no lease for that part. And no fixed deadline applies: the officer must be satisfied that you have taken up residence.

Citizenship is not domicile A US citizen who has lived abroad since childhood may have no adult US domicile to point to: no US earned income, no state ID, no utility record, perhaps no Social Security number and no voting record. That rules out four of the seven items on the Department's list from a standing start. If that is you, begin at the I-130 stage, not at the interview.
If you are the petitioner on a K-1 K visa applicants file an I-134 at the interview, not an I-864, so this section does not describe your paperwork. It does describe your risk. A petitioner outside the United States weakens the public charge picture, and the claim that you will marry and live there. The full I-864 domicile requirement applies when your fiancé(e) adjusts status. You defer it; you do not avoid it.

The NVC checklist, the binder checklist and the interview checklist all carry the domicile items.

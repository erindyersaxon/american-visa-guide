Life in the USA

What to do once you arrive, from your first 30 days to getting ready for citizenship, and how to find London members near you.

Here's what to do from here. Stages 7, 8 and 9 of the guide cover each step in detail.

  • Get your passport back by courier or from Mail Boxes Etc: Stage 7.
  • Pay the USCIS Immigrant Fee ($235), ideally before you travel, so the card's 90-day clock starts at entry: Stage 7.
  • Book a flexible flight. You travel on your immigrant visa, not an ESTA, and must enter before the visa's expiry date: Stage 8.
  • Confirm your US address at the port of entry and in your myUSCIS account, because USCIS mails the green card there. Until it arrives, the I-551 stamp in your passport is your proof of status, and you must carry it: Stage 9.
  • Expect your SSN card within 10 business days if you selected it on the DS-260. If it does not come, visit your local SSA office: Stage 9. Community timelines for both cards are below.

Your first 30 days Weeks 1–4

Start with the deadlines: your address, Selective Service if it applies, and health cover. Then get ID and a bank account.

Tell USCIS your new address within 10 days of every move. The law requires it of every permanent resident, and a willful failure can make you deportable. Use the USCIS online change of address: it meets the legal requirement, and it updates a pending case, such as an I-751, when you enter that case's receipt number. Enter every receipt number you have.

  • Moving from a temporary first address, such as family or a short let, counts.
  • Update SSA, your bank and your state DMV too. Each keeps its own record.
  • USCIS mails your green card, I-751 notices and N-400 appointments to the address it holds. A missed notice can cost you months.

Sources: USCIS: How to change your address · INA §265 (8 U.S.C. §1305) · INA §237(a)(3)(A)

Required by law for males aged 18–25

By law, male immigrants must register with the Selective Service System within 30 days of their 18th birthday. If they arrive aged 18 to 25, they must register within 30 days of entering the United States. This applies to legal permanent residents entering on an immigrant visa.

Registration does not mean military service. It is a legal administrative requirement. Failure to register can affect eligibility for federal employment, student loans, and naturalization.

Exempt: those who were on a valid non-immigrant visa continuously until age 26. Immigrant visa holders (CR1, IR1, IR2, IR5 etc.) are not exempt: registration is required.

Register at sss.gov/register: takes a few minutes online. [SSS: Who needs to register]

Arrange health cover in your first weeks. Moving to the US from another country qualifies you for a Special Enrollment Period, which usually gives you 60 days from the move to enroll, so you can buy a Marketplace plan outside the November to January open enrollment. Green card holders can buy Marketplace plans and may qualify for lower premiums based on income.

  • Start at HealthCare.gov. Some states run their own marketplace, such as Covered California and the Massachusetts Health Connector, and HealthCare.gov sends you there.
  • If your spouse has cover through work, ask their HR team whether you can join now. Each plan sets its own rules for adding a spouse outside open enrollment.

Visiting the UK later? NHS access changes once you live abroad: see Healthcare if you visit or return to the UK.

Sources: HealthCare.gov: Special Enrollment Period · HealthCare.gov glossary: Special Enrollment Period · HealthCare.gov: Coverage for lawfully present immigrants

Your first priority for US identification is a state driver's license or state ID. [DHS: REAL ID] Until then, your green card and your passport with the visa stamp work as ID. You still need a state ID for many everyday things. The same applies in a US territory (Puerto Rico, US Virgin Islands, Guam, American Samoa or the Northern Mariana Islands). Territories issue their own IDs, which are valid US identification.

Real ID vs Standard ID

Real ID is federally compliant. TSA has required it (or another accepted ID such as a passport or green card) at airport security since 7 May 2025, and federal facilities require it for entry. It requires more documentation than a standard ID.

Standard ID is valid for driving and general purposes but cannot be used to board domestic flights or enter federal buildings. Standard cards carry a marking such as "Federal Limits Apply" or "Not for federal identification". If you have a passport or green card you can fly on that, but a Real ID saves you carrying either.

Since 1 February 2026, TSA charges $45 through its ConfirmID process if you arrive with no accepted ID. The fee covers 10 days of travel, is non-refundable, and does not guarantee TSA can verify you.

Sources: TSA: REAL ID · Houston Airports: TSA to charge $45 fee

Proof of lawful presence
  • Permanent Resident Card (I-551)
  • Temporary I-551 stamp in passport (the visa stamp counts)
  • Employment Authorization Document (EAD)
Proof of Social Security Number
  • SSN card (cannot be laminated)
  • W-2 form displaying 9-digit SSN
  • Pay stub with name and SSN
  • SSA-1099 form
Proof of state residency
  • Utility bill, bank statement, or credit card statement (within 60 days)
  • Current lease or mortgage
  • Pay stub dated within 60 days
  • W-2 or tax statement
  • For Real ID: two residency documents required
Name match requirement

Your name must match across all documents. If your name has changed (e.g. marriage), bring a marriage certificate issued by a municipality, divorce decree, or court document. This is required for Real ID; not required for a standard license. If your green card shows a shortened name, bring your birth certificate or marriage certificate to show your full legal name.

Standard licenses without proof of lawful presence (19 states and DC)

The National Conference of State Legislatures (NCSL) counts 19 states and the District of Columbia that issue a standard license to residents who cannot prove lawful presence: California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Hawaii, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Minnesota, Nevada, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, Oregon, Rhode Island, Utah, Vermont, Virginia and Washington. Minnesota enacted the most recent law, in 2023. The National Immigration Law Center (NILC) lists the same jurisdictions and republished its state-law table in August 2025. Applicants in these states prove identity with documents such as a foreign passport, foreign birth certificate or consular ID, plus proof of state residency. [NCSL brief, updated 13 Mar 2023] [NILC: Driver's Licenses]

Why it matters: in these states you can often get a standard license on your UK passport before your SSN or green card arrives. Congress set lawful-status evidence as a REAL ID standard in 2005, so a Real ID still needs the documents listed above. Upgrade once your green card and SSN are in hand.

Driving across state lines: Florida (2023), Wyoming (July 2025), New Hampshire (September 2025) and Tennessee (January 2026) treat certain out-of-state licenses as invalid: those issued in a class reserved for people without lawful presence, or marked to show the holder did not prove it. These laws mainly reach separate card classes, such as Delaware's and Utah's driving privilege cards, rather than standard licenses that citizens also hold. If you hold one, replace it with a full license once you have your green card. [Fla. Stat. 322.033] [NHPR, Aug 2025] [Courthouse News]

Massachusetts snapshot

The Work and Family Mobility Act (in force 1 July 2023) lets you apply for a standard Class D license with a foreign passport or consular ID, plus proof of date of birth and Massachusetts residency; no SSN needed. You book two appointments: the learner's permit exam ($30), then the road test ($35); the license costs $50. Start at mass.gov/ID. [MassDOT RMV, 2023]

Requirements and fees vary by state and change. Check your state's DMV website for the exact documents before you go.

Opening a US bank account is one of the first practical priorities. You need it for direct deposit, bill payments, and to pay the USCIS immigrant fee.

  • Most major banks (Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, Citi) accept permanent residents. Bring your passport with visa stamp or green card, SSN, and proof of address. If your SSN has not arrived, some banks let you open the account now and add it later. Permanent residents qualify for an SSN, so you cannot use an ITIN instead.
  • Consider a credit union if you are new to the area, often more flexible for new residents
Building US credit

Your UK credit history does not transfer. Start with a secured credit card: you deposit a fixed amount as collateral, use the card for small regular purchases and pay the full statement each month. After 6–12 months of on-time payments you can apply for unsecured cards. Carrying a balance costs interest and does nothing for your score.

That said, you may not have to start from zero. Members have arrived with US history as an authorized user on their spouse's card, or by moving a UK Amex card to the US. See Preparing for US credit before you move.

Your first year Months 1–12

Build a US record: driving history, credit and your first US tax return, which also covers your UK accounts.

US car insurance is priced largely on your record in the US. With no US history, insurers usually rate you close to a new driver, even after decades of driving in the UK. That first-year premium is one of the bigger surprise costs of moving. Your UK record does not transfer automatically, and no US insurer has to count it. Some will, if you can show them documents. It rarely closes the gap, but the paperwork is free and takes ten minutes.

Download your DVLA driving record before you leave the UK

Use the DVLA View or share your driving licence information service. [GOV.UK: View or share your driving licence information] To sign in you need three things:

  • Your driving licence number
  • Your National Insurance number
  • The postcode DVLA holds on your licence

The record shows each category you hold and the date you got it, which proves how long you have driven. It also shows any endorsements or penalty points. Download the PDF summary and keep it. A check code for gov.uk/check-driving-information lasts only 21 days and works once.

Do this while you still have your UK address on the licence. DVLA does not put an overseas address on a GB licence, and signing in depends on the postcode it holds for you. So this gets harder once you have moved. Northern Ireland licences use the DVA equivalent via nidirect instead. [nidirect: Driving licences]

Also get a letter of experience from your UK insurer

US insurers generally care more about claims history than licence history, and the DVLA record does not show claims. Before you cancel your UK motor policy, ask your insurer for a letter of experience (sometimes called proof of no-claims discount) on company letterhead, covering:

  • The dates of continuous cover and the named policyholder
  • Any claims made, with dates, or explicit confirmation that there were none
  • Years of no-claims discount earned

Chasing one later from a US time zone is avoidable work. If you were a named driver rather than the policyholder, ask for confirmation of that too.

What to realistically expect
  • Insurers and states differ. Online quote forms have no field for UK experience. Speak to an agent, send the documents, and ask each insurer whether it counts overseas driving.
  • Time matters more. Six to twelve months of continuous US cover with a clean record moves the price more than any UK paperwork. Avoid a lapse in cover, which makes the next renewal worse.
  • The record shows points too. Answer questions about your history accurately: a misstatement can void a claim.

Some DMVs also ask for prior driving history, though none must waive a test because of it.

Your UK credit history and score do not carry over. Experian, Equifax and TransUnion in the US keep separate files from their UK counterparts. You can still start a US file before you arrive, so you land with months of history instead of none.

Become an authorized user on your spouse's US card

Ask your US citizen spouse to add you as an authorized user on a card they have paid on time for years. You get your own card on their account. If the issuer reports authorized users to the bureaus, the account appears on your US credit file with its payment history. [Capital One: Do authorized users build credit?]

Read the terms on liability. The CFPB says being an authorized user generally does not obligate you to pay the debt. Regulation B still lets an issuer require an authorized user to become contractually liable for the account, as long as it applies that rule to everyone. If the terms say so, you owe the debt alongside your spouse. The same rule bars the issuer from restricting who your spouse adds on a prohibited basis, such as national origin or marital status. [CFPB: Am I liable as an authorized user?] [CFPB: Comment for 1002.7, 7(a)-1 and 7(a)-2] [CFPB: Credit cards]

  • Start early. A FICO score needs one account open for at least six months and one account reported in the last six months. Add yourself six months or more before you move and you can have a score on arrival. [myFICO: Minimum requirements for a FICO Score]
  • Ask the issuer whether it reports authorized users to all three bureaus. The CFPB says issuers usually report them, but practice varies by issuer.
  • Pick the right card. Choose your spouse's oldest card with a clean record and a low balance. Their late payments and high balances land on your file too.
  • Use the same name and date of birth you will give the SSA and your bank. Some issuers ask for an authorized user's SSN; ask whether yours will add you without one and let you add it later.

Keep your spouse's card on the account after you arrive. Closing it or removing you can take its history off your file.

Titus: authorized user for 8 months before moving

Titus became an authorized user on his wife's cards before he left for the US, choosing the ones with a perfect payment history. Experian, Equifax and TransUnion began reporting the accounts on his file.

Once his SSN arrived, he opened a Chase bank account. Chase matched his name and date of birth to that file and showed him a VantageScore in the Chase app of around 750, built on 8 months of clean history. Two weeks later he applied for a basic no-annual-fee Amex card. Amex approved him within minutes, while he was unemployed. He could not qualify for an annual-fee card without income, but he expects the history to open up better cards later.

One member's result. Your starting score depends on the age, balances and payment record of the cards you join. Chase Credit Journey shows a VantageScore 3.0 from Experian data; most lenders decide on a FICO score, which can differ. Chase: VantageScore ranges explained

Once you have your own card: pay on time, every month

Ranger, a community member, puts it this way: to build credit on a card in your own name, make on-time payments each month. A Netflix subscription on the card, with autopay set to clear the full statement balance, does the job.

Payment history carries the most weight in both scoring models. FICO groups your file into five categories: payment history (35%), amounts owed (30%), length of credit history (15%), new credit (10%) and credit mix (10%). VantageScore uses six factors and also puts payment history first. [myFICO: What's in my FICO Scores?] [Chase: VantageScore 3.0 vs 4.0]

Amounts owed comes second, so keep the balance the card reports low against its limit. Paying the full statement on autopay covers both.

Earlier in the process? Open a UK Amex card now

If your spouse has no US card to add you to, or you have a year or more before you move, open an American Express card in the UK and use it as normal. Once you live in the US, apply for a US Amex card through Amex's Global Card Relationship service. Amex describes it as a Card Member-only service for applying for a new card in select countries. You apply as an existing Card Member instead of as a stranger with no US file. [American Express: Global Card Relationship]

  • Apply in the UK while you still live there. Amex UK runs its own UK credit check and needs a UK address.
  • Pay it in full every month. A clean record on the UK card is the relationship you bring with you.
  • Check the rules before you rely on it. Amex decides which countries and cards qualify and how long you must have held the card, and it can change them. Confirm with Amex before you move.

The new US card reports to the US bureaus like any other US card, so your US file and score start from the day Amex opens it. Keep the UK card open until the US card arrives.

A member's route: UK Amex to US Amex

One member opened an Amex card in the UK in March 2025 and used it as normal until moving to the US in January 2026. After the move, the member applied for a US card through the Global Card Relationship service and Amex approved the application straight away.

In September 2026, eight months after arriving, the member and their wife bought a house, which involved a credit check. In November 2026 the member's US credit score stood at 730.

One member's result. Amex sets its own approval criteria, and your score depends on how you use the card after it opens.

US employers, universities and licensing boards often ask for a credential evaluation of a degree you earned outside the US. An evaluator reviews your degree certificate and transcripts and issues a report stating the US equivalent, such as a US bachelor's or master's degree. Getting one before you need it costs money up front and saves you a scramble when a job offer or application lands.

Use a NACES member

Choose an evaluator that belongs to the National Association of Credential Evaluation Services (NACES). Many universities and employers accept evaluations only from NACES members. [NACES: Current members]

World Education Services (WES) is the name most people know. Community members report poor experiences with WES and recommend these two NACES members instead:

  • Center for Educational Documentation (CED)
  • International Education Evaluations (IEE)
Evaluations for immigration

IEE also runs a separate credential evaluation service for immigration. IEE aims it at temporary worker visas such as the H-1B and at employment-based petitions, where the petition relies on a foreign degree. IEE accepts legible scans for immigration evaluations and says its reports follow AACRAO EDGE. [IEE: Immigration services]

On a family-based immigrant visa you do not need one. A document-by-document or course-by-course report can still help with public charge: education and skills is one of the five factors the consular officer weighs, and the report tells the officer what your degree equals in the US. [9 FAM 302.8]

Where to send the report

You can have the evaluator send the report straight to an employer or university, or send it to you. If you hold your own copy and a school or employer later wants an official copy direct from the evaluator, you reorder and pay for another one.

  • Ask the school or employer which type of evaluation it wants before you order. A course-by-course report lists each subject and grade; a document-by-document report states only the degree equivalent. Universities usually want course-by-course.
  • Check how long the evaluator keeps your file. IEE says its evaluations do not expire, but it stops issuing copies five years after the original evaluation date. [IEE: FAQ]
Employer background checks

Some employers run background checks that use a third party to verify your degree with your university. An evaluation already on file does not replace that check, but it gives HR a US equivalent to work from and answers the first question they will ask.

Tax

Do not underestimate the tax and financial side

Becoming a US person triggers worldwide income reporting, FBAR [FinCEN: FBAR] and FATCA [IRS: FATCA] obligations, and potential PFIC exposure on UK investments. These are commonly missed and carry significant penalties. Address them early.

Welcome to the IRS

As a green card holder you must file US taxes annually and report worldwide income, regardless of where income is earned or whether tax was already paid in the UK. A tax treaty generally prevents double taxation, but you still must report everything. For the first year, use a tax preparer experienced in international/expat filings.

If your total combined foreign account balances exceed $10,000 at any point in the year (cumulative across all accounts, not per account), you must file an FBAR. This covers all UK bank accounts, ISAs, and investment accounts.

  • Report all foreign account details, highest balance during the year, and year-end balance
  • File at: bsaefiling.fincen.treas.gov
  • This is one of the most commonly missed obligations and carries significant penalties
  • Consolidate UK accounts before moving where possible and maintain clear balance records

If you hold foreign financial assets over approximately $50,000 (varies by filing status), you must also file Form 8938. This covers foreign bank accounts, investments, and certain pensions. More info: irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-8938

If you receive large foreign gifts (including from family in the UK), this may need to be reported via Form 3520. It is a reporting requirement, not necessarily a tax. More info: irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-3520

Many UK-based US tax preparers do not support clients after relocation. Register with an expat specialist early, before your first US filing year, and expect higher costs for that first year. It is worth it to get it right.

Travel

Global Entry is a US Customs and Border Protection (CBP) program that allows pre-approved travelers to clear customs faster when entering the United States. As a permanent resident, you are eligible to apply. British citizens can participate. It is worth doing!

What you get
  • Use dedicated Global Entry kiosks at US airports. Skip the regular customs queue entirely
  • Includes TSA PreCheck automatically, no separate application needed. Means expedited security screening at US domestic airports: no removing shoes, laptops, or liquids
  • Makes re-entry to the US significantly faster, especially at busy US airports like JFK and LAX. Also available at US preclearance in Dublin and Shannon (there is no US immigration inspection at Heathrow). Can use at land borders re-entering the U.S. from Canada
Cost and eligibility
  • Fee: $120 for a 5-year membership
  • Children under 18 are free: they can be added to a parent's account at no extra cost
  • Many US credit cards cover the application fee as a benefit. Check cards like Chase Sapphire, Amex Platinum, Capital One Venture, and Citi cards. If you or your US citizen spouse hold one of these, the fee is likely covered
  • Permanent residents are eligible: green card holders can apply regardless of nationality

Source: CBP: Global Entry

How to apply
  1. Create an account at ttp.cbp.dhs.gov (Trusted Traveler Programs portal)
  2. Complete the online application: takes around 20 minutes. Provide travel history, employment, and background information
  3. Wait for conditional approval, typically a few days to a few weeks
  4. Schedule an in-person interview at a Global Entry Enrollment Center. Alternatively, your Global Entry enrollment interview can be completed without the need to schedule an appointment at an Enrollment Center. Enrollment on Departure (EoD) and Enrollment on Arrival (EoA) are two convenient options.
  5. Bring your green card, passport, and any other documents listed in your approval notice
  6. Note that flight bookings must include your Known Traveler Number aka your Global Entry number on your booking to get the benefit!

Processing times vary. Applying early in your LPR status gives more time before your next international trip. As an LPR you will receive a physical Global Entry card (eligible British citizens who are not LPRs can apply, but Global Entry is tied only to the passport. Not all nationalities who are not LPRs can apply).

Keeping your green card Years 1–2

You keep permanent residence while the US stays your home. CR1 holders also file Form I-751 in the 90 days before the 2-year card expires.

Key forms at a glance
Form I-131 Reentry Permit: traveling for 1+ year Form I-751 Remove conditions: CR1/CR2 holders Form I-90 Renew or replace your green card Form N-470 Preserve residence: qualifying jobs abroad only
CR1 / CR2 holders only

If you entered on a CR1 visa (married less than 2 years at the time of US entry), you received a 2-year conditional green card. You must file Form I-751 (Petition to Remove Conditions on Residence) to upgrade it to a permanent 10-year green card. If you do not file, your conditional status terminates automatically when the card expires.

Filing window: do not miss this

You must file I-751 during the 90-day window immediately before your 2-year green card expires. Look at the "Card Expires" date on your green card and count back 90 days. That is your earliest filing date.

  • Filing even one day too early: USCIS will reject and return the petition
  • Filing after expiry: your conditional status ends. USCIS accepts a late petition only with a written explanation showing good cause for the delay (8 CFR 216.4(a)(6)). Do not rely on this
What happens after you file
  • USCIS sends a Form I-797C receipt notice, which extends your conditional green card for 48 months past its expiry date. You can live, work and travel while the petition is pending.
  • Processing time as of 2026: typically 21–24 months, with some cases longer
  • USCIS has increased scrutiny in 2026 and may require an in-person interview

Source: USCIS: Form I-751

Traveling while your I-751 is pending

Your 2-year card will often expire before USCIS decides your I-751. You can still travel. Carry the originals of both documents together:

  • Your expired conditional green card
  • Your Form I-797C receipt notice for the I-751

Sources: USCIS: Form I-751 and I-829 48-Month Extension · USCIS: Removing Conditions Based on Marriage

Before you book
  • Other countries may not accept the pair. Airlines and foreign border officers may not recognize an expired card plus a receipt notice. Check the entry and transit rules for every country on your route.
  • Watch for USCIS appointments. USCIS may schedule biometrics or an I-751 interview while you are away. If you miss the interview without good cause, USCIS can terminate your conditional residence (8 CFR 216.4(b)(3)).
  • Trips of a year or more need a reentry permit. After a year abroad, your card and receipt notice no longer work for re-entry (8 CFR 211.1(a)(2)). File Form I-131 while you are still in the US. For shorter absences and how USCIS judges abandonment, see Maintaining your permanent residency status.
  • Get advice first if your record has problems. CBP decides admissibility at the port of entry. If you have a criminal record or earlier immigration violations, speak to an immigration attorney before you leave.

Source: USCIS: Form I-131. General information only, not legal advice.

I-751 and naturalization can overlap

You can file Form N-400 (naturalization) while your I-751 is still pending. You do not need to wait for I-751 approval before applying for citizenship, as long as you are otherwise eligible. Filing the N-400 can prompt USCIS to decide the I-751 sooner.

I-751 checklist
  • Form I-751 (a joint petition with your spouse, or a waiver), filed in the 90-day window
  • A copy of your conditional green card, front and back, and the fee from the USCIS fee schedule
  • Passport-style photos, if filing by mail
  • Evidence the marriage is genuine: joint bank statements, a joint lease or mortgage, joint tax returns, insurance naming each other, children's birth certificates, dated photos over time, travel records, and affidavits from people who know you as a couple
  • If divorced: the decree, or evidence that proceedings have begun

Your green card grants lawful permanent resident (LPR) status, but that status can be lost if you abandon it. The key question USCIS and CBP ask is: do you intend to make the United States your permanent home?

Travel outside the US: what you need to know
  • Under 6 months: generally fine, the green card is valid for re-entry
  • 6 months to 1 year: CBP may question whether you have abandoned residence. Be prepared to show strong US ties (employment, home, tax filings, family)
  • Over 1 year: your green card alone will not get you back in. Before you leave, apply for a Reentry Permit (Form I-131) while physically in the US, 3 to 6 months ahead. You need a copy of your green card, a passport-style photo and the fee, and USCIS schedules biometrics. The permit lasts 2 years.
  • Over 2 years: a reentry permit will have expired. You may need to apply for a Returning Resident Visa (SB-1) at a US Embassy

Source: USCIS: International Travel as a Permanent Resident

What USCIS looks at to assess abandonment
  • Duration and frequency of absences from the US
  • Whether you maintained a US home, employment, and bank accounts
  • Whether you filed US income taxes as a resident
  • Whether your immediate family remained in the US
  • Whether you held a valid US driver's license and maintained a US mailing address
  • Whether you owned property or ran a business in the US
Reentry permit does not protect naturalization eligibility

A reentry permit keeps your green card, not your naturalization clock. An absence of more than 6 months can disrupt continuous residence, and a year or more breaks it. Form N-470 preserves it only for qualifying work abroad, such as for the US government, a US research institution or certain US firms, filed before the absence reaches a year. Source: USCIS Form I-131 Instructions

Green card renewal (Form I-90)

10-year green cards should be renewed using Form I-90 before they expire. USCIS recommends filing 6 months before expiry. An expired card does not mean you have lost LPR status, but it may affect employment verification and travel. Source: USCIS: Form I-90

Once you are a US permanent resident living in the US, NHS access changes. The NHS is residence-based, not citizenship-based: you lose free access once you move abroad permanently.

NHS charges for non-residents
  • A&E (Accident & Emergency): Always free: anyone can attend A&E in the UK regardless of where they live
  • GP (family doctor): Charged to overseas residents, typically £80–200+ per visit
  • Hospital treatment: charged for overseas residents, and costs vary from hundreds to thousands of pounds depending on treatment
  • Prescriptions: Standard prescription charges apply (£9.90 per item as of 2026), or overseas residents may be charged private rates

Source: NHS: Visiting or moving to England

If you return to live in the UK

You regain free NHS access immediately upon re-establishing UK residence. Register with a GP as soon as you return. There is no waiting period for residents. Source: gov.uk: Using the NHS when you return to live in the UK

Healthcare planning
  • Arrange private health insurance in the US before you move. Do not rely on NHS access for ongoing healthcare
  • If you return to the UK temporarily (holidays, visiting family), plan for potential medical costs: travel insurance may help but often excludes pre-existing conditions
  • Check both UK and US insurance requirements when planning long-term healthcare

Getting ready for citizenship Years 2–5

USCIS checks your trips, taxes and record from the years before you file the N-400. Start the habits now. Your biological or adopted children under 18 may already be citizens.

  1. Work out your filing date. Most permanent residents wait 5 years. If you are married to and living with a US citizen who has been a citizen for the whole period, you wait 3. You can file up to 90 days early. Filing date calculator [INA §316(a), §319(a); 8 CFR 334.2(b)]
  2. Keep a travel log from your first trip. The N-400 asks about your trips outside the US over the whole residence period. Note every departure and return date as you go, even for short trips, and check it against CBP's I-94 travel history.
  3. Plan long trips around continuous residence. USCIS presumes a trip of 6 months to a year broke it unless you can prove otherwise, and a trip of a year or more breaks it unless you qualify for and filed Form N-470. You also need to spend at least half the period inside the US. Continuous residence · Physical presence [8 CFR 316.5(c)]
  4. File US taxes every year as a resident. If you claim nonresident status on a federal or state return, or skip filing because you consider yourself a nonresident, USCIS can presume you gave up permanent residence. Why taxes matter [8 CFR 316.5(c)(2)]
  5. Never vote or claim to be a US citizen. Decline voter registration at the DMV, and leave the citizen box unchecked on job and benefit forms. Voting in any election limited to citizens, or a false claim to citizenship for any purpose under federal or state law, can make you deportable and end your path to citizenship. [INA §237(a)(3)(D), §237(a)(6)]
  6. Settle before you file. USCIS needs you to have lived in the state or USCIS district where you file for the 3 months immediately before you file. State residence [8 CFR 316.2(a)(5)]
  7. Keep the earlier steps clean. Men who had to register with Selective Service should keep their confirmation. CR1 holders can file the N-400 while the I-751 is still pending.

General information only, not legal advice. If you have a criminal record or a past immigration problem, speak to an immigration attorney before you file.

IR2 / CR2 children under 18

If your minor children came with you on IR2 or CR2 visas, they may already be US citizens automatically, by law. The exception is when the US citizen is their stepparent (see below). Under the Child Citizenship Act of 2000 (INA §320), a child born outside the US automatically acquires US citizenship the moment all of the following are true:

  • At least one parent is a US citizen (by birth or naturalization), and the child is that parent's biological or adopted child
  • The child is under 18
  • The child is a lawful permanent resident (green card or I-551 stamp)
  • The child resides in the US in the legal and physical custody of the US citizen parent
Stepchildren do not become citizens automatically

For citizenship purposes, the law defines a "child" as a biological child or one adopted under the statute's conditions. It does not include stepchildren (INA §101(c)(1)). A CR2 or IR2 visa can be issued to the US citizen's stepchild, but holding one does not make the child a citizen. A stepchild stays a permanent resident, and becomes a citizen only by one of two routes:

  • Adoption by the US citizen parent, generally before age 16, followed by two years of legal custody and residence with that parent. INA §320 can then apply while the child is under 18.
  • Naturalization on Form N-400 at 18 or over, once otherwise eligible.

A child born to an unmarried US citizen father generally has to have been legitimated to count as his child. If either applies to your family, speak to an immigration attorney before applying for a US passport for the child.

No application is needed to acquire citizenship. It happens automatically when the last condition is met. What you do need is proof. There are two ways to document it: a US passport (Form DS-11, through the Department of State) and a Certificate of Citizenship (Form N-600, through USCIS). The passport is faster and cheaper; the certificate never expires and is the permanent record. Many families start with the passport.

Sources: US Department of State: Obtaining US Citizenship under the Child Citizenship Act · USCIS Policy Manual, Vol. 12, Part H, Ch. 4: Automatic Acquisition of Citizenship after Birth (INA 320) · USCIS Policy Manual, Vol. 12, Part H, Ch. 2: Definition of Child · 8 U.S.C. §1101(c)(1)

Applying for the child's US passport (Form DS-11)

Apply in person at a passport acceptance facility: most commonly a USPS post office. Both parents/guardians must appear with the child, or the absent parent must provide a notarized Form DS-3053 consent. Per the State Department, submit:

  • Proof of relationship to the US citizen parent: certified copy of the child's foreign birth certificate (with translation if not in English)
  • Proof of LPR admission: the child's permanent resident card, or foreign passport with I-551 stamp
  • Evidence of the US citizen parent's citizenship (e.g. US passport or naturalization certificate) and photo ID
  • Evidence the child resides in the US with the US citizen parent: the green card alone is not proof of residence. Provide at least two documents such as a lease or rental agreement naming the household, school enrollment records, or medical/doctor records
  • Completed Form DS-11, passport photo, and fees

Fees: $135 total for a child's passport book ($100 application fee paid to the Department of State plus $35 execution fee paid to the acceptance facility). Expedited service is an additional $60. Routine processing is typically 4–6 weeks; expedited 2–3 weeks, plus mailing time. Check the current fee chart before applying.

Sources: State Department: Apply for a Child's Passport (Under 16) · State Department: Passport Fees

Community experience: send the physical green card
  • Members report being told at the post office that a photocopy of the green card is enough, then getting a request for the original, which delayed the application by weeks. The State Department requires originals or certified copies, plus photocopies. Send the child's physical green card with the application, even if the acceptance agent says a copy will do. You will then have to mail it separately if it is requested later
  • Once the child's citizenship is documented, the green card no longer reflects their status. Do not expect to rely on it again
  • Including a copy of your lease or rental agreement showing the child living at the US citizen parent's address has worked well as residence evidence
  • Community timelines: passports received in roughly 3–4 weeks with expedited service, around 6 weeks routine

Community-reported experience, 2026. Official document requirements: State Department: Citizenship Evidence

Certificate of Citizenship (Form N-600), optional but permanent

The passport proves citizenship but expires (5 years for under-16s). A Certificate of Citizenship from USCIS never expires and is the definitive lifetime record that citizenship was acquired under INA §320. It is not required, and the filing fee is substantial. Check the current fee before deciding. Many families obtain the passport first and file the N-600 later, or keep careful certified copies of the underlying evidence instead.

Source: USCIS: Form N-600, Application for Certificate of Citizenship

Child's first US passport: checklist
  • Wait until the child has their physical green card (and ideally their SSN card) in hand
  • Form DS-11. Do not sign until instructed by the acceptance agent
  • Child's certified foreign birth certificate + translation if needed (original and photocopy)
  • Child's physical green card (original and photocopy of front and back)
  • US citizen parent's proof of citizenship and photo ID (originals and photocopies)
  • Two or more documents showing the child lives with the US citizen parent (lease/rental agreement, school records, medical records)
  • Both parents present, or notarized DS-3053 from the absent parent
  • Passport photo, application fee ($100 check to US Department of State) and $35 execution fee, plus $60 if expediting

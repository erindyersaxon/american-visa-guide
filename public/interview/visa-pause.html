US Embassy London · Nationality visa pause

The nationality visa pause has been struck down

On 21 August 2026 a federal court vacated the State Department policy that suspended immigrant visas for nationals of 75 countries. The ban was triggered by nationality, not by the embassy you interviewed at, so if you interviewed at US Embassy London on a non-UK passport and were caught by the pause, this is the decision that undoes it. Here is what it does, what it does not do, and how to read it yourself.

This page explains the pause, the ruling and what embassies have been told. Each hearing, cable and order since is on embassy and visa news. The ruling is under appeal, so check the latest there before you make plans you cannot undo. Called back after a 221(g)? Go to re-interview after a 221(g).

Bottom line The nationality-based immigrant visa ban is void. The court set aside refusals made only because of it, and sent them back to consular officers to decide again on the facts. That covers everyone affected, not just the people who sued. It is not an automatic visa, and a refusal that had a separate, genuine ground still stands.

What the pause was

On 14 January 2026 the State Department announced that, effective 21 January 2026, it would suspend the issuance of immigrant visas to nationals of 75 countries, on the theory that immigrants from those countries posed a high risk of becoming a “public charge.” A cable from Secretary of State Marco Rubio ordered consular officers to refuse those applicants even where the officer had already determined, individually, that the person was not a public-charge risk and was otherwise eligible for the visa.

In practice the policy worked as a blanket ban based on nationality, covering nearly 40% of the world’s countries, whatever the person’s circumstances.

What the court decided

In Catholic Legal Immigration Network, Inc. (CLINIC) v. Rubio, No. 26-cv-00858, the US District Court for the Southern District of New York (Judge Jeannette A. Vargas) held the policy contrary to law and beyond the Secretary of State’s authority, and vacated it under the Administrative Procedure Act. The court found the ban violated:

ProvisionWhy the ban broke it
8 U.S.C. § 1201(g) Requires an individualized eligibility assessment by a consular officer. The ban ordered officers to refuse applicants they had found eligible.
8 U.S.C. § 1152(a)(1)(A) Prohibits discrimination in issuing immigrant visas based on nationality. The ban did exactly that. It sorted applicants by their passport.
22 C.F.R. § 40.6 A visa may be refused only on a ground specifically set out in law. “Nationality on a list” is not such a ground.
8 U.S.C. § 1104(a) Strips the Secretary of State of authority over the consular adjudication of individual immigrant visas. The policy overrode that discretion.

What the order does:

  • Cancels the policy entirely. The court rejected the government’s argument that relief should cover only the named plaintiffs: vacatur under the APA nullifies an unlawful policy in full, for everyone, not just those who sued.
  • Sets aside refusals based only on the ban, and sends those cases back to consular officers to decide again. A refusal may not rest on the policy. The court does not dictate the outcome: only that the ban cannot be the reason.
  • Declares the policy unlawful, which makes it harder for the agency to reissue the same ban in a new form.
Important limit The vacatur does not reach a refusal where the officer found the applicant ineligible on some other ground, for example a genuine individualized public-charge finding, a missing-document 221(g), or another inadmissibility: even if the refusal paperwork also cited the ban. If your refusal named a real, case-specific reason, that reason still stands and still has to be resolved.

What has happened since

The ruling did not restart anyone's case by itself. The State Department cancelled immigrant visa interviews worldwide for public charge training. The plaintiffs asked the court to enforce the judgment, and on 10 September the Department withdrew the guidance behind the pause. Each hearing, cable and order, in date order, is on embassy and visa news.

What embassies have been told

Cable 26 STATE 88862 (10 September 2026) rescinds the pause in full. No one may be refused under 221(g) because of it. Posts must:

  • find every pause refusal and work them oldest first;
  • write to applicants for anything missing, including new medicals;
  • give the specific reason for any fresh 221(g);
  • complete the public charge worksheet in every immigrant visa case.

The cable expects some applicants to attend a second interview. A call-back does not signal a problem.

Who goes first

Cable 26 STATE 89798 (14 September) and a Visa Office message of 21 September set the queue. Posts take named plaintiffs and Chief of Mission exception cases first, then everyone else by refusal date. A manager reviews a share of decisions each day, and approved cases go through a further review (IVAR) before the visa prints.

If a post replies that your case is “being reviewed in the order in which they were refused,” it is using the Department's set wording.

Unconfirmed: applicants report that families of US service members and government staff go first. No filed document says so. Unless a Chief of Mission exception request went in for your case, do not count on it.

Why pause cases move and new interviews do not

Cable 26 STATE 86164 (1 September) requires each consular section to finish in-person public charge training before it processes immigrant visas. On 5 October the Department told the CLINIC court that pause cases are exempt from the in-person part. No public document says when London finishes its training. For whether London is sending interview letters yet, see embassy and visa news.

Where each cable sits on a public docket: 26 STATE 88862 is Medani v. Trump ECF 95-1; 26 STATE 86164 and 26 STATE 80666 are Medani ECF 115-2 and 115-3 (with a Consular Affairs declaration at ECF 115-1); 26 STATE 89798 and the Visa Office message are CLINIC ECF 114-2 and 114-3 (declaration at ECF 114-1). Treat any copy of a cable that is not on a docket as unverified.

If you interviewed in London and are not a UK national

This is the key point for London cases: the ban was triggered by your country of nationality, never by the embassy you attended. US Embassy London processes many immigrant-visa applicants who live lawfully in the UK but hold a different passport: third-country nationals. If that other nationality was one of the 75 listed countries, your case was caught by the pause at London exactly as it would have been anywhere else in the world.

  • If you are a British national, the pause never applied to you. This decision does not change your case.
  • Did you interview at London on the passport of a listed country, and get refused, held, or told the visa “could not be approved at this time” because of the pause? Then this ruling helps you.
  • London calls pause cases back for a full interview. Bring your passport and all your documents. Expect public charge questions, and check whether anything has changed since your refusal, above all whether the petitioner still lives in the US. See re-interview after a 221(g).
  • If you hold a second, unaffected nationality, that route was always open. Make sure the unaffected passport is uploaded to CEAC. With the 75-country pause vacated, that pause is no longer a bar. The separate travel ban may still be. Proclamation 10998, in force since 1 January 2026, suspends immigrant visas for nationals of 39 countries. It removed the old exception for immediate relatives of US citizens. This ruling did not touch it. If your nationality is on that list, the pause ruling does not help you; the proclamation has its own limited exceptions (including, as reported, dual nationals travelling on a passport of a country not on the list), so check them before relying on a second passport.

What it does not mean

  • It is not an automatic visa. Vacatur restores the prior status quo: your case goes back to a consular officer for a normal, individualized decision. A visa still never guarantees admission at the US port of entry.
  • It is a trial-court ruling under appeal. The government appealed to the Second Circuit. Check the latest on embassy and visa news before booking flights.
  • Public charge still applies. Officers now use a worksheet and new training, and managers review decisions.
  • It does not fix a separate problem. If your refusal rested on anything other than the pause, treat that as the live issue.
  • It does not lift the travel ban. Proclamation 10998 (effective 1 January 2026) separately suspends immigrant visas for nationals of 39 countries, with no general exception for spouses, children or parents of US citizens. The 21 August ruling addressed only the 75-country policy. Many nationals were caught by both; for them, vacating the pause changes nothing until the proclamation is lifted or an exception applies.

What to do now

  1. Find out exactly why you were refused. Pull your 221(g) sheet or refusal notice and read the stated ground. If the only reason was the nationality suspension, your case falls within this decision and should be re-processed.
  2. Separate the pause from everything else. If the refusal gave any other reason, such as finances, a document or an inadmissibility, deal with it the normal way. See our 221(g) and public charge pages.
  3. Do not start again. The court set your refusal aside. Nothing needs refiling and there is no new fee.
  4. Renew stale documents now. Medicals and police certificates expire. Three named plaintiffs left their October interviews with a new 221(g) because their medical had run out.
  5. Prepare for public charge questions. The officer fills in a worksheet covering all five statutory factors. See public charge. If you get a new 221(g), the officer must state the specific reason: see 221(g).
  6. Wait for instructions from the embassy on how to proceed. Your case goes back to the post that refused it, and the embassy will write to you with anything it needs. There is nothing to refile and no new fee. Keep watching the email address on your case and your AIS account, and see what embassies have been told. London has given at least one applicant less than 24 hours' notice of a new date, so check daily. Because the government has appealed, follow the docket (linked below) before making irreversible plans.
  7. Get advice for anything complex. A prior refusal, an inadmissibility finding, or an unusual fact pattern is worth an AILA-affiliated attorney.

Public charge: what you must prove, and how

Updated 1 October 2026. What changed.

The rules changed. Officers now test public charge in every case.

Until late 2025, a sufficient Form I-864 settled the public charge question in most cases. It no longer does. The officer must be satisfied, on five factors about you, the beneficiary, that you will not depend on public support. The law puts the burden of proof on you (INA 291).

  • November 2025. The State Department told officers to weigh each factor in earnest, instead of treating a sufficient I-864 as enough.
  • 15 August 2026. The Department sent a public charge worksheet to every post. Officers work through it factor by factor.
  • 1 September 2026. Mandatory public charge training and expanded supervisor review of decisions took effect worldwide.
  • 10 September 2026. Cable 26 STATE 88862 told every post to complete the worksheet in every immigrant visa case.

London cancelled its September 2026 interviews to train officers on this. Applicants interviewed since then report 35- to 40-minute interviews, officers checking with a supervisor, and requests to see bank statements and property deeds at the window.

What this means for you
  • Treat the statement as mandatory. The embassy says you may send one. Send it, with its evidence, whatever your circumstances.
  • Expect no benefit of the doubt. The officer will not fill gaps for you. A document you cannot produce or a figure you cannot give leads to a 221(g) refusal, and clearing one takes months.
  • You answer the questions, not your sponsor. The petitioner is not in the room. Learn their income, debts and insurance as well as your own.

1. The five factors the officer weighs

The statute sets five factors (INA 212(a)(4)(B)). Each one is about you. The officer still asks about the petitioner's age, dependents and debts, because those shape how far the household income stretches. Your statement needs a block for each factor.

The five factors, and what your statement must show
FactorWhat the officer looks forWhat you show
Age Enough working years to fund your own retirement. Applicants under 16 or of advanced age draw closer scrutiny. The petitioner's age matters too: a sponsor near retirement has fewer years to deliver the income. Your age, working years ahead, pension provision, who supports any minor, and the petitioner's income after retirement if it is close.
Health The panel physician's report, and any condition with high lifetime cost: diabetes, heart disease, cancer, neurological or serious mental health conditions. Officers may also note visible risk factors such as obesity. The condition, how you manage it, its US cost in dollars, who pays, and how you stay insured if an employer plan ends.
Family status Who depends on the household income. Posts count everyone the petitioner supports, including relatives abroad, which is wider than the I-864 count. They ask whether any dependent has a condition under treatment or a long-term care need. Everyone in the household, their ages, the income that supports them, and who funds any care. Who counts.
Assets, resources and financial status Recurring household income first. Savings, property and retirement accounts count after it, with debts set against them. Officers may verify what you claim. Income that pays each cost, then savings as a buffer. Assets do not replace income.
Education and skills Qualifications, licences and work skills, and whether US employers and regulators will recognize them. English proficiency carries particular weight. Your highest qualification, years in your field, any relicensing step with a timeline, and your English fluency.

Sources: INA 212(a)(4); 9 FAM 302.8; CLINIC's analysis of the unpublished November 2025 cable, State Department Redefines Public Charge Standard.

3. Questions to rehearse

Answer each of these aloud, and make each answer match your statement. Officers ask them out of order and return to anything that does not satisfy them. Practise with the flashcards.

The petitioner and any joint sponsor

  • What does the petitioner do, for whom, and what do they earn before and after tax?
  • How old is the petitioner? If they are near retirement, what income continues?
  • If they are self-employed: what is the business, and what will they do if it fails?
  • Why was their income low in a given tax year? The officer has the transcript in front of them.
  • Is their job unionised, or does it carry a pension and benefits?
  • Do they have a mortgage, credit card debt or car loans?
  • Why do you have a joint sponsor? What do they do, how do they know you both, and have they sponsored anyone before? See section 6.

Your work

  • Who do you work for, what does the company do, and when did you join? Officers have searched an applicant's employer online during the interview.
  • Is your work remote? Will your employer let you work from the US? If not, what is your plan?
  • What will you do for work in the US, and when?
  • What did you study, where, and what other certifications do you hold?
  • Do you need a US licence to work in your field? Who supports you while you get it, and for how long?

Money

  • What do you earn? Give the figure in US dollars.
  • What savings, property and investments do you hold? Officers ask to see printed proof.
  • Do you have other income, including cash-paid work? Describe it accurately.
  • What debts does your own household carry, including UK student loans and child support, and what happens to each after the move?

Health, insurance and housing

  • Which health plan covers you, from when, and what covers you until then?
  • Do you have an ongoing condition? What does its treatment cost in the US? One London applicant was asked whether she had priced insulin.
  • Where will you live, and which income pays the rent or mortgage and the premiums?

The household

  • Who lives at the address, and who else does the petitioner support?
  • Does any dependent have a health condition or care need, and who pays for it?
  • Has anyone in the household received means-tested benefits or used a food bank? See section 5.
What one London interview looked like

A beneficiary who sent a statement in advance described her 2026 interview:

  • It lasted 35 to 40 minutes. Before the change, applicants reported approvals in two minutes or less.
  • The officer had read her statement and medical report, and consulted a supervisor throughout.
  • She had to explain her husband's below-threshold tax year and what he would do if his new business failed.
  • She was asked to show her savings and proof of two properties. She could evidence one.

Each unusual feature of her case drew its own line of questioning. List the features of yours, and prepare an answer and a document for each. Read her full account.

4. Where cases go wrong

Savings do not replace income at London

London has refused cases built on assets, including some that met the I-864 asset test on paper, and required a joint sponsor anyway. Applicants report this across many cases; the embassy has not published it. Make recurring income the answer to every cost in your statement, and list savings last as a buffer. If the sponsor's income sits at or near 125% of the Federal Poverty Guidelines, get a joint sponsor. Use the I-864 household decision tree.

The failures applicants report most often
What goes wrongWhat to do
Your answers contradict the statement, because you never saw the sponsor's figures Go through section 3 aloud with the petitioner before the interview
The statement arrives too late for anyone to read it Send it at least five working days ahead
You name an asset you cannot prove at the window Bring printed proof of every asset you name, or leave it out
You assume a remote job survives the move Get your employer's written permission. Without it, describe the job as one you will seek
The sponsor had a weak tax year and you cannot explain it Know the cause, what changed, and what happens if it recurs
You treat a health condition as a non-issue State how you manage it, its US cost, and who pays
Your qualifications need relicensing in the US Name the evaluation body or exams and your timeline
You assume clearing NVC means your finances passed NVC checks completeness only. Prepare as if no one has reviewed your finances. See how to prevent a 221(g)
You cannot document cash or informal income Describe it accurately, do not inflate it, and lead with income you can document
UK student loans: disclose them

The officer weighs debts against income, and a student loan is a debt. State the plan type, the balance in pounds and dollars, and the expected monthly repayment in dollars with the income that pays it. Send your latest Student Loans Company statement.

Moving abroad does not cancel the loan. If you will live outside the UK for more than three months, tell the Student Loans Company before you leave and complete its overseas income assessment. If you skip it, the Company charges a fixed monthly repayment and may apply its highest interest rate. See GOV.UK: repaying your student loan.

5. Benefits and care at public expense

Officers consider any support you received because your income or savings fell below a threshold, in any country, including charity. Posts' written questionnaires name food banks. Answer for yourself, the petitioner and any joint sponsor, now and in the past.

  • Give the context: when, why, for how long, and what changed. The builder and template have a block for this.
  • Personal Independence Payment does not count, but it points to a health condition. Prepare the health answer: how you manage it, and who pays in the US.

6. Sponsors and the I-864

Clearing 125% of the poverty guidelines gets the I-864 accepted. It does not settle public charge.

The officer looks behind the figure, at the sponsor's financial history, their own benefit use, and whether they will make the money available to you. One rule still runs the other way: where an I-864 is required, a missing or insufficient one sinks the case on its own.

  • Know why you have a joint sponsor, in one true sentence. Officers ask. "We weren't sure" invites more questions. Form I-864 requires a tax return for the most recent year only (Part 6); a short US tax history is not a reason by itself. Send three years where you have them.
  • Describe the joint sponsor's relationship, how long they have known each of you, where they live, and whether they have sponsored anyone before. An earlier I-864 obligation still in force raises the income they must show.
  • The petitioner's US domicile still has to hold. A joint sponsor cannot cover for it. See the domicile problem.
  • Every sponsor now authorizes a credit check on the 08/24/26 edition of the I-864. Sponsors should read their own credit reports before signing, lift any credit freeze when asked, and be ready to explain defaults or large debts.

7. How to submit

Upload to CEAC first, then email. Do both.
  1. Make one PDF per document type, each under 2 MB, named so the officer can tell what it is: 01_Public_Charge_Statement.pdf, 02_Petitioner_IRS_Tax_Returns_2023-2025.pdf, 03_Petitioner_Payslips_Jul-Sep_2026.pdf, 04_Petitioner_Bank_Statements_Jul-Sep_2026.pdf, 05_Petitioner_Health_Insurance.pdf, 06_Joint_Sponsor_IRS_Tax_Returns_2023-2025.pdf, 07_Beneficiary_Student_Loan_Statement.pdf. List them in the same order at the end of the statement.
  2. Upload them to your case in CEAC. Log in at ceac.state.gov/IV with your case number and invoice ID, and use SUBMIT DOCUMENTS, in red at the bottom of the page.
  3. Email the same files to LNDIVSubmissions@state.gov so the unit knows to look. Subject line: Public Charge Statement – LND0123456789 – FULL NAME. If the files are too large for one email, number the emails (1 of 3, 2 of 3) under the same subject.
  4. Finish at least five working days before your interview. Work out the date.

Posts confirm a complete submission by email and do not expedite cases. Once you have sent it, leave it alone.

Public charge: what you must prove, and how

Updated 23 October 2026. What changed.

The rules changed. Officers now test public charge in every case.

A sufficient Form I-864 no longer settles the question. The officer looks at five factors about you, the beneficiary, and must be satisfied you will not depend on public support. The law puts the burden of proof on you (INA 291). Since August 2026 each officer works through a public charge worksheet, and supervisors review decisions. London cancelled its September interviews to train officers on it. Dates and court filings.

  • Treat the statement and an evidence pack as mandatory. The embassy says you may send a statement. Send it, with a labelled pack of documents behind every claim in it, for you as well as your sponsors. The pack.
  • Expect no benefit of the doubt. A document you cannot produce or a figure you cannot give can lead to a 212(a)(4) refusal. You can overcome one with new evidence; nobody has published how long that takes.
  • Lead with income. Savings back it up; they do not replace it. A salary or pension pays every month for as long as you live in the US, and savings run down. London has refused cases that met the I-864 asset test on paper. Why.
  • You answer the questions, not your sponsor. Learn the petitioner's income, debts and insurance as well as your own.

1. The five factors the officer weighs

The statute sets five factors (INA 212(a)(4)(B)). Each one is about you. The officer still asks about the petitioner's age, dependents and debts, because those shape how far the household income stretches. Your statement needs a block for each factor.

The five factors, and what your statement must show
FactorWhat the officer looks forWhat you show
Age Enough working years to fund your own retirement. Applicants under 16 or of advanced age draw closer scrutiny. The petitioner's age matters too: a sponsor near retirement has fewer years to deliver the income. Your age, working years ahead, your retirement plan, who supports any minor, and the petitioner's income after retirement if it is close.
Health The panel physician's report, and any condition with high lifetime cost: diabetes, heart disease, cancer, neurological or serious mental health conditions. Officers may also note visible risk factors such as obesity. The condition, how you manage it, its US cost in dollars, who pays, and how you stay insured if an employer plan ends.
Family status Who depends on the household income. Posts count everyone the petitioner supports, including relatives abroad, which is wider than the I-864 count. They ask whether any dependent has a condition under treatment or a long-term care need. Everyone in the household, their ages, the income that supports them, and who funds any care. Who counts.
Assets, resources and financial status Recurring household income first. Savings, property and retirement accounts count after it, with debts set against them. Officers may verify what you claim. Income that pays each cost, then savings as a buffer. Assets do not replace income.
Education and skills Qualifications, licences and work skills, and whether US employers and regulators will recognize them. English proficiency carries particular weight. Your highest qualification, years in your field, any relicensing step with a timeline, and your English fluency.

Sources: INA 212(a)(4); 9 FAM 302.8; CLINIC's analysis of the unpublished November 2025 cable, State Department Redefines Public Charge Standard.

In detail: who counts as a dependent

Posts' written questionnaires count all of the petitioner's dependents, and list them: minor children, spouse, parents, or extended family and friends you are financially supporting. Form I-864 has no equivalent of that last clause. It builds household size from tax dependents and sponsored immigrants.

A petitioner who sends money to a parent abroad, or supports a sibling or friend, has a dependent for the questionnaire and none on the affidavit. Both counts can be correct. Work out each one before the interview, and prepare to explain the difference. For the affidavit's arithmetic, use the I-864 household decision tree.

2. The evidence pack

Assume the officer wants proof of everything. Moderators who prepare members for interviews report that officers now ask the beneficiary for financial documents too, not just the petitioner. They ask for bank accounts, assets in your home country, and a CV and diplomas to show your work history. A statement on its own gives the officer claims to test. Send the documents that prove them.

  • Send every row that applies to your case. Skip a row only when the thing does not exist for you: no joint sponsor, no property, no debts.
  • Back every figure. If the statement names an income, asset, debt or cost, the pack holds the document behind it.
  • Label each file with its code, one PDF per row, and list the codes at the end of the statement. The officer can then ask for a file by name.
  • Upload it to CEAC, email it, and print it. The printed copy goes in your interview binder under the same labels. How to submit.

Why send all of it before anyone asks? In September 2026 an officer at US Embassy Warsaw asked for the petitioner’s bank statements and insurance proof at the interview, with no warning. Moderators who prepared members for re-interviews in October 2026 report requests for the beneficiary’s CV, diplomas, bank statements and home-country assets. The list also matches the public charge checklist Consulate General Lagos printed on a 221(g) form. A 221(g) refusal holds the case open for missing documents. A 212(a)(4) refusal is a finding that you are likely to become a public charge. The joint sponsor rows follow a 212(a)(4) refusal letter from Rio de Janeiro.

If anyone is self-employed

This applies to the petitioner, a joint sponsor or you. A payslip shows a salary arriving every month. Self-employed income has no payslip and changes month to month, so officers look harder at it. In the London interview account, the petitioner’s new business led to questions on what it does and what he would do if it failed.

  • 12 months of bank statements instead of 3, from the business bank account if there is one. The officer then sees a full year of income coming in. Add personal statements if the business pays into a personal account.
  • Proof the business is registered: the state business registration or certificate of formation, and any business or professional licence.
  • Proof it trades: a Better Business Bureau profile if it has one, the business website, recent invoices, client contracts, or Forms 1099.
  • Tax returns with every schedule: the IRS transcripts in P01 or J01 should show the business income, such as Schedule C for a sole proprietor. Know the gross and net figures, and why any year was low.

File it as P09, J05 or B12, and put a sentence in your statement on what the business does and how long it has run.

Your retirement plan: officers now ask the beneficiary

Moderators report that officers ask beneficiaries how they will fund retirement. The officer wants one of two answers, or both, and you should name the plan in your statement:

  • You will work in the US and earn Social Security. A US retirement benefit needs 40 credits, about 10 years of work (SSA: how you earn credits). Under the US–UK social security agreement, UK National Insurance years can count toward the US total once you have at least 6 US credits. Say what work you will do and for how long.
  • You have another plan. Name it: a workplace pension, a private retirement plan or personal pension, investments, property. Give the provider and the value in dollars, and put the latest statement for each in file B13.

Add your UK State Pension forecast from GOV.UK: check your State Pension forecast. The UK pays the State Pension to you in the US. Officers weigh this answer under the age factor: working years ahead, and what supports you after them.

3. Questions to rehearse

Answer each of these aloud, and make each answer match your statement. Officers ask them out of order and return to anything that does not satisfy them. Practise with the flashcards.

Other officers may observe your interview. Beneficiaries at embassy interviews, including in London, increasingly report other officers observing the interviewing consular officer. At some posts five or six officers have stood behind the interviewing officer; one member counted eight. No post has explained it. It fits the supervisor review and public charge training described under the worksheet, the training and the cable. Answer the officer in front of you, at your own pace.

The petitioner and any joint sponsor

  • What does the petitioner do, for whom, and what do they earn before and after tax?
  • How old is the petitioner? If they are near retirement, what income continues?
  • If they are self-employed: what is the business, and what will they do if it fails?
  • Why was their income low in a given tax year? The officer has the transcript in front of them.
  • Is their job unionised, or does it carry a pension and benefits?
  • Do they have a mortgage, credit card debt or car loans?
  • Why do you have a joint sponsor? What do they do, how do they know you both, and have they sponsored anyone before? See section 6.

Your work

  • Who do you work for, what does the company do, and when did you join? Officers have searched an applicant's employer online during the interview.
  • Is your work remote? Will your employer let you work from the US? If not, what is your plan?
  • What will you do for work in the US, and when?
  • What did you study, where, and what other certifications do you hold?
  • Do you need a US licence to work in your field? Who supports you while you get it, and for how long?

Money

  • What do you earn? Give the figure in US dollars.
  • What savings, property and investments do you hold? Officers ask to see printed proof.
  • Do you have other income, including cash-paid work? Describe it as it is.
  • What debts does your own household carry, including UK student loans and child support, and what happens to each after the move?
  • What is your plan for retirement? Will you work in the US long enough to earn Social Security, or do you have another plan? Name it: a workplace or private pension, investments, property. How to answer.

Health, insurance and housing

  • Which health plan covers you, from when, and what covers you until then?
  • Do you have an ongoing condition? What does its treatment cost in the US? One London applicant was asked whether she had priced insulin.
  • Where will you live, and which income pays the rent or mortgage and the premiums?

The household

  • Who lives at the address, and who else does the petitioner support?
  • Does any dependent have a health condition or care need, and who pays for it?
  • Has anyone in the household received means-tested benefits or used a food bank? See section 5.
In detail: one London interview, 35 to 40 minutes

A beneficiary who sent a public charge statement in advance gave us this account of her 2026 interview at US Embassy London. Her profile drew extra questions: she works in a niche digital finance field, has Type 1 diabetes, owns property and earns cash from a family business. Read it for how the officer worked.

  • It lasted 35 to 40 minutes, and she cut it short to make a hotel checkout. Before the change, applicants reported approvals in 40 seconds to two minutes.
  • The officer consulted a supervisor throughout. That is the wider supervisor review described under the worksheet, the training and the cable, in the room.
  • The officer had read her statement and her medical report, and had run a background check. Officers read what you send.
  • The officer questioned the sponsor's finances in depth. Her husband's 2025 income on the tax transcript fell below the minimum because he had opened a business. The officer asked her why, what the business does, and what he would do if it failed, then whether he belongs to the union at his workplace and whether he has a mortgage.
  • Her own job took up most of the interview. The officer asked who she works for, when she joined, who employed her before, whether her work is remote, whether her company would let her work from the US, and her plan if it would not. The officer searched her employer online and read the results back to her.
  • The officer asked to see evidence. She showed printed bank and investment statements for her savings. Asked for proof of two properties she owns and co-owns with family, she could prove one.
  • The officer probed her cash income. She helps run her mother's property management company and takes cash in place of a salary. The officer wanted to know how the arrangement works, step by step.
  • The health questions turned on cost. The officer asked about her insurance and whether her husband's plan covered her, then whether she knew the US price of insulin and had looked into it.

Your interview may run shorter. Each unusual feature of a case opens its own line of questions: a new business, a weak tax year, a chronic condition, remote work, co-owned property, cash income. The officer follows each line until satisfied. List the features of your case and prepare an answer and a document for each.

4. Where cases go wrong

Savings support your case. They do not replace regular income.

The I-864 lets assets fill an income shortfall, and the officer weighs assets under the financial factor. At London that has not been enough on its own. London has refused cases built on assets, including some that met the I-864 asset test on paper, and required a joint sponsor anyway. Applicants report this across many cases; the embassy has not published it.

The officer asks what pays the rent, the insurance and the food in the second year and the fifth. A salary or a pension answers that. A savings balance shrinks each month you draw on it.

  • Make recurring income the answer to every cost in your statement: a salary, a pension, or rental income with a lease behind it.
  • List savings last, as a buffer for emergencies, and say how many months they would cover.
  • If the sponsor's income sits at or near 125% of the Federal Poverty Guidelines, add a joint sponsor rather than more savings. Use the I-864 household decision tree.
  • The same holds for children and parents: see sons and daughters and parents.
The failures applicants report most often
  • Your answers contradict the statement, because you never saw the sponsor's figures.
    Go through section 3 aloud with the petitioner before the interview.
  • The statement arrives too late for anyone to read it.
    Send it at least five working days ahead.
  • You name an asset you cannot prove at the window.
    Put proof of every asset you name in the evidence pack and in your binder, or leave the asset out.
  • You assume a remote job survives the move.
    Get your employer's written permission. Without it, describe the job as one you will seek.
  • The sponsor had a weak tax year and you cannot explain it.
    Know the cause, what changed, and what happens if it recurs.
  • You treat a health condition as a non-issue.
    State how you manage it, its US cost, and who pays.
  • Your qualifications need relicensing in the US.
    Name the evaluation body or exams and your timeline.
  • You assume clearing NVC means your finances passed.
    NVC checks completeness only. Prepare as if no one has reviewed your finances. See how to prevent a 221(g).
  • You cannot document cash or informal income.
    Describe it as it is, do not inflate it, and lead with income you can document.
UK student loans: disclose them

The officer weighs debts against income, and a student loan is a debt. State the plan type, the balance in pounds and dollars, and the expected monthly repayment in dollars with the income that pays it. Send your latest Student Loans Company statement.

Moving abroad does not cancel the loan. If you will live outside the UK for more than three months, tell the Student Loans Company before you leave and complete its overseas income assessment. If you skip it, the Company charges a fixed monthly repayment and may apply its highest interest rate. See GOV.UK: repaying your student loan.

6. Sponsors and the I-864

Clearing 125% of the poverty guidelines gets the I-864 accepted. It does not settle public charge.

The officer looks behind the figure, at the sponsor's financial history, their own benefit use, and whether they will make the money available to you. One rule still runs the other way: where an I-864 is required, a missing or insufficient one sinks the case on its own.

  • Know why you have a joint sponsor, in one true sentence. Officers ask. "We weren't sure" invites more questions. Form I-864 requires a tax return for the most recent year only (Part 6); a short US tax history is not a reason by itself. Send three years where you have them.
  • Describe the joint sponsor's relationship, how long they have known each of you, where they live, and whether they have sponsored anyone before. An earlier I-864 obligation still in force raises the income they must show.
  • The petitioner's US domicile still has to hold. A joint sponsor cannot cover for it. See the domicile problem.
  • Every sponsor now authorizes a credit check on the 08/24/26 edition of the I-864. Sponsors should read their own credit reports before signing, lift any credit freeze when asked, and be ready to explain defaults or large debts.

Not a spouse? The I-864 treats a child’s or parent’s own income differently, and some children need no I-864 at all. See section 7 for children, sons and daughters on IR2, and section 8 for parents on IR5.

In detail: what posts ask about a joint sponsor

With a joint sponsor, the written questionnaire adds a block of questions. One consulate's 221(g) checklist also asks for a more detailed description of how the joint sponsor is related to the petitioner. The joint sponsor does not attend, so you answer for them. Prepare these:

  • Why you have one. At another post an officer asked: why did you add a joint sponsor if your wife's income was enough? Give a true answer in one sentence. "The petitioner's income was close to the threshold" works. So does "the petitioner had only recently returned to US employment." "We weren't sure" invites the next question.
  • The relationship, described. "My husband's colleague" is a label. Say how they know each other, for how long, and how often they speak. An officer who cannot place a joint sponsor gives their support less weight.
  • How long they have known the petitioner and you. The questionnaire asks about both in one question. A sponsor who has never met you still qualifies. Say so up front.
  • Whether they live in the same state as the petitioner. Distance does not bar them. The officer uses it to judge whether the sponsor would hand over the money.
  • Whether they have sponsored anyone else for an immigrant visa. An earlier I-864 obligation that has not ended counts toward the joint sponsor's household size on your I-864, and raises the income they must show. Check before you file, with the I-864 household decision tree.
  • Whether they have used public assistance. Officers weigh their benefit history as they weigh the petitioner's.

If you have spoken to your joint sponsor only once or twice, have a proper conversation before the interview.

A short US tax history does not call for a joint sponsor

In the case above, the couple added a joint sponsor because the petitioner lacked three years of US tax returns. Form I-864 asks, yes or no, whether you filed a return for each of the last three tax years. It is easy to read that as a requirement. The note under that question in the 08/24/26 edition says you must attach a transcript or photocopy for only the most recent tax year. It adds that you may submit three years if you believe they help (Form I-864, Part 6, Item Numbers 15 to 19, and their instructions). Send three years where you have them, because they show sustained income. Once you file a joint sponsor's I-864, the officer examines their details in the same depth as the petitioner's.

In detail: the I-864 credit check

In the 08/24/26 edition of Form I-864, each sponsor signs a new authorization in Part 8. It lets USCIS and the State Department obtain credit reports and scores from consumer reporting agencies in connection with the sufficiency determination of the affidavit.

  • It lasts through a 221(g). The authorization runs until the officer approves the case, the one-year window to overcome a refusal closes, or the sponsor withdraws the I-864. It stays valid during any period in which an application is pending administrative processing.
  • A credit freeze can delay your case. The form warns sponsors that a security freeze may block the government's access, and asks them to respond to any request to lift it.
  • The government can share negative findings with you, so that you can respond under 8 CFR 103.2(b)(16).

Each sponsor, joint sponsors included, should read their own credit reports before signing, and prepare to explain any defaults, collections or large debts. The officer may ask you about the petitioner's debts.

In detail: income before and after tax

The I-864 uses gross income, and compares it against 125% of the Federal Poverty Guidelines. Written public charge questionnaires ask for the petitioner's annual salary after taxes, because the officer wants to know whether the household can absorb another person. Have both numbers, and say which one you are quoting. An officer who expects gross and hears net may read it as a contradiction of the I-864.

7. Children of US citizens (IR2)

Children under 21

This page assumes an adult spouse who works. A child joining a US citizen parent faces the same five factors, but the I-864 rules and the evidence differ. Nobody has yet sent us an IR2 interview account from London, so this section rests on the statute and the form, not on what officers have asked. For a child aged 21 or over, see sons and daughters aged 21 or over.

  • Many children under 18 need no I-864. A child becomes a US citizen on admission under INA 320 if they are under 18, unmarried, and will live in the US in the legal and physical custody of their citizen parent. That child does not file Form I-864. The exemption covers the form, not the public charge test, so expect the officer to ask who supports and insures the child.
  • Confirm with NVC how to claim the exemption. USCIS removed Form I-864W, the old exemption form, from its website in February 2025. Applicants inside the US now claim the exemption on Form I-485. We have found no published instruction for immigrant visa cases, so ask NVC through its Public Inquiry Form before you submit documents.
  • The exemption fails in four cases. A stepchild does not meet the definition of child for citizenship (INA 101(c)). A child who will live abroad, or with someone other than the citizen parent, does not acquire citizenship on arrival. Nor does a married child, or one aged 18, 19 or 20. Each of these needs a full I-864. For a child aged 18 or over, the parent's assets need cover only three times any income shortfall.
  • Each child is a separate case. A child's petition carries no derivatives, so each sibling needs their own I-130 and I-864. The sponsor's dependent children under 21 count in the household size wherever they live.
  • Age: say who looks after the child day to day, and whose income pays for it.
  • Education: written questionnaires ask a school-aged child's current grade, when they expect to graduate, and whether they plan further study. Name the US school or school district.
  • Health: send proof that the parent's health plan covers dependents, and when the plan would add the child. Price any ongoing treatment in dollars, as for an adult.
  • Family status: list everyone the parent supports, including the child's other parent if the petitioner pays them support.

Use the builder. Choose “Child under 18” in the statement builder. It writes the statement in your voice, as the petitioner, on the child's behalf.

Change the evidence pack. Rows B01 (CV), B06 (employer letter) and B13 (retirement) do not fit a child. Send instead:

  • B14: the child's full birth certificate, naming the citizen parent;
  • B15: a custody order, or the other parent's written consent to the move, if the parents live apart;
  • B16: the latest school report, and proof of a US school place or the district the child will attend;
  • P04: the parent's health plan document, showing it covers dependents.

Sources: INA 101(c) and 320; Form I-864 instructions, edition 08/24/26, pages 2, 12 and 14 (exemptions, assets, household size); USCIS Policy Manual, Volume 8, Part G, Chapter 6 on claiming the exemption on Form I-485.

Sons and daughters aged 21 or over

This page assumes a spouse. If a US citizen parent petitioned for you as a child and you are now 21 or over, most of it still applies, with one difference on the I-864. Nobody has yet sent us an IR2 interview account from London, so this section rests on the statute and the form, not on what officers have asked.

  • Check your category. For an immediate relative, the Child Status Protection Act fixes your age on the date your parent filed the I-130 (INA 201(f)(1)). If you were under 21 that day, you stay IR2. If not, your case moved to F1. The public charge test is the same in both.
  • You need a full I-864. Only a child under 18 who becomes a citizen on arrival under INA 320 is exempt. The officer assesses you as an adult, on all five factors, and every beneficiary row of the evidence pack applies to you as written.
  • Your own income may not count toward the 125% line. A spouse's income counts wherever the spouse lives. The I-864 instructions let any other relative count their own income only if it continues from the same source after the move and they live with the sponsor now. If you live apart from your parent, their income, or a joint sponsor's, has to clear the threshold without yours. If you live together and your parent counts your income, send proof of your address as row B17.
  • Your own savings can close a gap on the form, not in the interview. Your assets count on the I-864 wherever you live, and need no Form I-864A. A citizen parent sponsoring a child aged 18 or over needs assets worth three times the shortfall, not the five times other relatives need. Example: if your parent's income falls $4,000 short of the line, $12,000 of savings between you closes it on paper. London has refused cases that met the asset test, so the officer still needs an income that pays your costs: your own job, your parent's, or a joint sponsor's. Treat savings as backup to recurring income, never a replacement for it.
  • Your independence still counts. The officer weighs your own income, savings, work history and qualifications in the five factors. Years of supporting yourself make a strong answer. Prove them with payslips, bank statements, your CV and an employer letter or US job plan.
  • Say you are not a dependent. The questionnaire counts everyone the petitioner supports with money. If you have supported yourself for years, say so in your statement, and say since when.
  • Health insurance depends on your age. A US employer plan that covers children must keep them until 26. If you are under 26, ask your parent's plan whether and from what date it would add you, and send that answer. From 26 you need your own plan: send the healthcare.gov quote in row B07.
  • Say where you will live. If you move in with your parent, the officer will ask who pays the rent and bills. If you will live alone, give your own budget: rent, insurance, and the income that pays them.

Sources: INA 201(f)(1) and 320; Form I-864 instructions, edition 08/24/26, pages 2, 12 and 14 (exemptions, assets, and the intending immigrant's income); 42 U.S.C. § 300gg-14 on dependent coverage to age 26.

8. Parents of US citizens (IR5)

A parent of an adult US citizen faces the same five factors as a spouse. Age and health weigh more, and the parent's own pension often cannot count toward the I-864. Nobody has yet sent us an IR5 interview account from London, so this section rests on the statute, the form and published benefit rules, not on what officers have asked.

The I-864

  • Your pension counts toward the 125% line only if you live with your child now. The I-864 instructions let a relative other than a spouse count their own income only if it continues from the same source after the move and they currently live with the sponsor. A parent living in the UK while the child lives in the US cannot count a State Pension or workplace pension toward the line. The officer still weighs it under assets and resources.
  • Your savings count wherever you live, at five times the shortfall, but they do not replace income. The instructions' own example is a parent: if the sponsor's income falls $4,000 short, the assets of the sponsor, any household member and the parent must reach $20,000 between them. London has refused cases that met the asset test on paper. A parent may live in the US for decades, and savings that cover five years leave the rest unanswered. Lead with income that keeps paying: your child's salary, your pensions, or a joint sponsor's income. Put savings after it, as a buffer. Why.
  • The sponsor's own family counts first. Household size includes the sponsor's spouse, their dependent children under 21 wherever they live, and you. A sponsor with a young family has less income to spare, and the officer will see it.
  • Two parents means two cases. Each parent has their own I-130 and I-864. Once the first parent immigrates, they count in the household size on the second parent's I-864. Plan for the income to cover both.
  • A sibling can be the joint sponsor. A brother or sister in the US who is a citizen or permanent resident, and meets 125% for their own household, is the usual answer when one child's income falls short.
  • The sponsor's duty can last for life. It ends when you become a citizen, earn 40 quarters of US work, or give up permanent residence, or when you or the sponsor dies. A retired parent rarely earns 40 quarters, so the duty usually runs until naturalization. Expect the officer to test whether the sponsor can carry it that long.

The five factors, for a parent

  • Age. You have few or no working years ahead, so the officer looks for retirement income in place of a job. A pension paid for life is regular income; a retirement pot you draw down is an asset, and counts as savings. Name each pension, its value in dollars, and confirm it will be paid to you in the US. Put the statements and your UK State Pension forecast in row B13.
  • Health. This carries the most risk. You can buy into Medicare only after 5 years of continuous residence as a permanent resident. With fewer than 30 quarters of US work, Part A then costs $565 a month in 2026 (CMS). Medicaid carries its own five-year bar. Until then you need a marketplace plan: get a healthcare.gov quote for your age and ZIP code (row B07), and price each medication in dollars (row B08).
  • Subsidies may not help. Since 2026, permanent residents with income below the poverty line who are barred from Medicaid by immigration status no longer qualify for marketplace premium tax credits (One Big Beautiful Bill Act, section 71302). A parent with little US income pays the full premium. Say who pays it.
  • Long-term care. Questionnaires ask whether anyone has been in long-term care at government expense, and Medicaid pays for most US nursing home care. Say who would pay for care if you could not live alone: family, savings or a long-term care policy.
  • Family status. Say whether you will live with your child, and who else depends on their income.
  • Education and skills. If you plan to work, say what and for how long. If you do not, say so, and let the pension answer carry the factor.

Questions to rehearse

  • Why are you moving now, and will you live with your child?
  • What pension do you receive, from whom, and how much in dollars? Does it continue in the US?
  • Do you own property in the UK? Will you sell it, and where does the money go?
  • What covers your health costs for the five years before Medicare, and what does your medication cost in the US?
  • Who will care for you if you can no longer live alone, and who pays?
  • If you have a joint sponsor: why, and how are they related to you?

The statement builder has a “Parent” option. If you are retired, it replaces the work section with your pensions and adds a block on care in later life. Rows B01 (CV) and B06 (employer letter) apply only if you will work. Rows B07, B08 and B13 carry the case. If your child counts your pension on the I-864, add proof that you live at their address now, as row B17.

Sources: Form I-864 instructions, edition 08/24/26, pages 10, 12, 14, 15 and 16 (previously sponsored immigrants, assets, household size and the intending immigrant’s income, and how long the obligation lasts); 42 U.S.C. §§ 1395i-2 and 1395o on Medicare for permanent residents; Kansas Health Institute on H.R. 1 and the marketplace.

10. If the officer refuses under 212(a)(4)

A 212(a)(4) refusal is a finding that you are likely to become a public charge. Unlike a 221(g), it is a decision on the merits, and no waiver exists. You overcome it with new evidence. If you send that evidence within one year of the refusal, the post must reconsider the case (22 CFR 42.81(e)). See 221(g) vs 212(a)(4).

What the Rio de Janeiro letter says
  • The officer decided on the totality of the circumstances: the I-864, any supporting documents, and the five factors of age, health, family status, financial resources, and education and skills.
  • You can overcome the refusal with new evidence of a change in circumstances, or by adding a joint sponsor.
  • To add a joint sponsor, the post asks for three things:
    • Form I-864 and Form I-864A, both signed. Form I-864A applies when a household member’s income counts toward the sponsor’s (USCIS, Form I-864A).
    • the most recent IRS tax transcript, with proof of US citizenship or permanent residence for each person whose income counts
    • a written statement of the relationship and history of support between you and the joint sponsor, with proof of their assets and how each converts to cash within one year
  • Upload the documents to CEAC and email them to the post, with the case number and the beneficiary’s name in the subject line.
What the London form says
  • It is a tick-box form. The officer marked Section 212(a)(4) and nothing else.
  • It gives no reason, names none of the five factors, and offers no remedy, document list or address. The remark lines are empty.
  • Both waiver boxes are blank. For an immigrant visa, 212(a)(4) has no waiver (INA 212(a)(4), 8 U.S.C. § 1182).

The two letters mirror each other. Rio explains its decision and leaves off the applicant’s name and case number. London’s form names the applicant, carries the case number and an officer’s signature, and explains nothing. If London refuses you under 212(a)(4), expect this form.

Expect to work out the reason yourself

The Rio letter names the five factors and one remedy, and leaves the rest to you. The London form gives no reason at all. The post will not tell you which documents would change its mind, and the burden of proof stays with you (INA 291). Your reconsideration package has to answer every concern the officer could have held.

Build the reconsideration package

  1. Audit your own case, factor by factor. List each weak spot the officer could have seen. For example: income near the threshold, a weak tax year, debts, a health condition with no dollar cost, no insurance quote, a job that ends with the move, limited English, or qualifications that need relicensing. Any liability or gap in your finances counts against you.
  2. Answer each weak spot with a document that shows action. The two main routes are a joint sponsor with a valid I-864, and financial documents that show extra income. Others: a health insurance quote, a remote-work letter or US job offer, a credential evaluation, enrolment in English classes.
  3. Rewrite your statement to say what has changed since the interview, factor by factor, and which file proves each change.
  4. Send the whole evidence pack with the new files added, labelled as before, so the reconsideration file stands on its own.
  5. Upload it to CEAC, then email it to the post, with your case number and full name in the subject line. Use the address on your refusal letter. London’s form prints none; London takes documents at LNDIVSubmissions@state.gov, the address on its 221(g) sheet.

The steps follow advice from community moderators who have helped members refused under 212(a)(4), and 212(a)(4) letters members have shared from several posts. Reconsideration is not guaranteed. If your case rests on a single income with no joint sponsor available, consider speaking to an immigration attorney.

In detail: the worksheet, the training and the cable
  • 15 August 2026. The Department sent consular officers a public charge worksheet for use at every post. Officers use it to weigh the totality of your circumstances.
  • 1 September 2026. Four new training requirements took effect. Two affect your case: wider supervisor review of consular decisions, and mandatory training on the public charge analysis. Both cover immigrant visa cases worldwide, beyond the cases the courts sent back.
  • 10 September 2026. Cable 26 STATE 88862 reminded every post to complete the worksheet in every immigrant visa case.

Your officer works through all five factors on a set form, and a supervisor may review the decision. Write your statement around the same five factors, and the officer finds each answer where the form asks for it.

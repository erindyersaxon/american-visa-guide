Stage 1: USCIS / I-130

⏱ IR visas: stalled at priority dates around 25 Feb 2025, now 19+ months and rising Reviewed 27 Sept 2026

Bottom line

You are here: You are starting the process. The petitioner, the US citizen or green card holder, files Form I-130 with USCIS for the beneficiary, the relative who will move.

What happens: The I-130 proves your family relationship qualifies. It does not look at your finances and it does not issue a visa. When USCIS approves it (the NOA2 notice), your case moves to the National Visa Center. [USCIS I-130] Nobody can give a reliable processing time for a case filed today: see Current status below. If your priority date is on or after 25 February 2025, read If your I-130 is delayed.

Do this first: Work through the I-130 pre-submission checklist, then file online.

Action list

  1. Petitioner: Create a free USCIS online account at myaccount.uscis.gov.
  2. Either: Work through the I-130 pre-submission checklist before you start the form. A complete, consistent package is the best thing you can do for your timeline.
  3. Beneficiary: For a spouse petition, fill in Form I-130A (Supplemental Information for Spouse Beneficiary). You do not need to sign it if you fill it in outside the United States.
  4. Petitioner: File online. Use the same exact legal names and dates everywhere. Upload clear scans, sorted by category, with file names that say what each one is.
  5. Petitioner: Save your NOA1 receipt notice, and add your priority date to the community tracker.
  6. Petitioner: Filing for more than one relative? Link the petitions yourself, because USCIS will not do it for you.

Documents for this stage

  • If your I-130 is delayed — only if your petition is well past processing times: how to push USCIS to decide
  • If your child is a US citizen (CRBA) — only if your child was born to a US citizen parent: a CRBA and passport instead of a visa

Current status: the queue has stopped at priority dates around 25 February 2025

Community tracking shows USCIS has largely stopped deciding I-130s for consular cases. USCIS has not announced a pause, and this is not official data. Decisions fell from mid-February 2026, and the front of the line has barely moved since. Track My Visa reports that the oldest undecided petitions have priority dates of around 25 February 2025. The little movement left is in cases filed before that date. Those petitions have now waited about 19 months, and each week of the stall adds to that and to your total wait. Until early February 2026, filing to decision took about 12 months. Do not plan around that figure. Treat any published 12-month estimate as a minimum from a period that has ended, not a forecast.

This stall is unusual. It is not a normal backlog. Few cases past the 25 February 2025 line have been approved. Community reports say nearly all of those escalated, with a demand letter or a writ of mandamus; the queue did not reach them in turn. If your priority date is on or after that line, read If your I-130 is delayed and the remedies and escalation guide. A writ forces a decision, not an approval, and escalation guarantees nothing.

The stall showed no sign of easing through summer 2026. It affects every consular case, whatever your nationality. It is separate from the 75-country visa pause, which a federal court struck down on 21 August 2026. AOS and DCF filings are not in this queue.

USCIS decides the I-130, not an embassy, so this stage runs the same wherever you file from. This site does not track it. Track My Visa does, across all consulates, with regular updates on the queue: go there for current I-130 numbers. The official USCIS quarterly figures lag behind the community data. Our national data section shows them from October 2023, next to State Department visa issuances.

1
File the I-130
File through your USCIS online account or by mail. Upload your civil documents and supporting evidence.
2
Receipt (NOA1)
USCIS accepts the case and gives it a priority date. IR visas: nobody can give a reliable processing time right now (see Current status above). Once approved, the case goes straight to NVC. F visas: your priority date must become current on the Visa Bulletin before NVC starts work. That can take years, depending on your category and country of chargeability. Check the Visa Bulletin every month.
3
Review
The case waits in the queue. Its status moves from "Case Was Received" to "Actively Being Reviewed", then to approval.
4
RFE (if issued)
USCIS asks for evidence that is missing or too weak. The clock stops until you respond. Sending strong evidence when you file makes an RFE much less likely.
5
Decision (NOA2)
If USCIS approves, the case goes to NVC. If USCIS denies, you can appeal to the Board of Immigration Appeals on Form EOIR-29, filed with USCIS within 30 days of the decision (33 days if mailed). Or you can file a new I-130.

We recommend filing online for consular cases. You get your NOA1 receipt at once, you upload documents instead of posting originals, and you can track the case in the portal. The steps below run in order, and each links to the detail.

1
Create a USCIS online account
The petitioner (the US citizen or permanent resident) creates a free account at myaccount.uscis.gov. You file, upload evidence, get notices and track the case here.
2
Gather documents and evidence first
Work through the I-130 Pre-Submission Checklist before you start the form. It covers your visa category, the civil documents you need, relationship evidence, and a check for RFE risks. A complete, consistent package is the best thing you can do for your timeline.
3
Complete the form sections
The online form asks for petitioner details, beneficiary details, five years of addresses and jobs, and family members (Part 4). Filing for more than one relative? See the linking guide below. Use the same exact legal names and dates everywhere: mismatches between forms are a leading cause of RFEs. For help field by field, see the resources at the end of this guide.
4
Upload your evidence
Upload clear scans, sorted by category, with file names that say what each one is. Clear labels and order matter more than volume.
5
Review, pay, and submit
Check every section against your documents. Then pay the filing fee by card or US bank account, and submit. Check the current fee with the USCIS fee calculator before paying.
6
Save your receipt and a copy of the filing
Your receipt notice (NOA1) and receipt number appear in the portal almost at once. Save it, and add your priority date to the community tracker. Download the PDF copy of your submitted I-130 for your records. The tip below says what to expect from that PDF.
7
What happens next
Your case joins the queue and follows the five steps at the top of this section: NOA1, review, a possible RFE, then NOA2. You need to do nothing unless USCIS sends an RFE. Check the portal now and then, and keep your address up to date.
Petitioner: Where to go for field-by-field help

This guide stays at the level of steps, because the form's questions and screens change over time. For detail, use the official USCIS I-130 page and instructions, and Kseniya International's online I-130 filing walkthrough for a screen-by-screen guide to the spouse petition.

Transgender and non-binary applicants

Under current US policy (April 2025), USCIS takes sex from the original birth certificate and recognizes only male or female. If your documents show different sex markers, expect delays at USCIS, NVC and the embassy medical. See Stage 5: Medical exam for full guidance and support resources.

Fixing typos or errors on your I-130

If USCIS made a typing error, or you spot a mistake on your I-130 after filing, ask for a correction with USCIS's e-Request for Typographical Error Correction. You can do this at any stage, before or after approval. Send evidence of the error and the correct information.

Downloading a PDF copy of your I-130

The "Download PDF" link in your myUSCIS portal gives you a copy of the I-130. It looks like the paper form and fills in what you submitted online. Some fields come out wrong. That is normal.

Filing I-130s for more than one family member, such as a spouse and a child? USCIS will not link them for you. Make the connection clear yourself, so the cases move through NVC together:

1
Label and upload receipt notices
Upload each I-130 receipt notice (NOA1) to the other family member's USCIS account. Give the file a clear label, such as "PLEASE ATTACH – FAMILY CONNECTION" or "FAMILY ATTACHMENT: Other Applicant's I-130".
2
Cross-upload identity documents
Upload the other applicant's birth certificate or passport to each I-130. For example, the child's birth certificate goes in the spouse's I-130, and the other way round.
3
Complete Form I-130 Part 4, Question 25
List the other applicant (spouse, child, stepchild) here. Already submitted? Upload a written amendment that states the family connection.
4
Upload supporting family evidence
On the I-130 filed first, upload a PDF of family evidence labeled "FAMILY UNIT EVIDENCE". For stepchildren, this could include photos of all three of you together, labeled with names and dates.
5
Ask USCIS to link the applications (optional)
Use the Emma chat tool at USCIS.gov to ask for a Tier 2 officer, and ask them to link the applications. The steps above should already make the connection, and USCIS may not answer the chat.
6
If one case gets stuck at NVC
If one case is at NVC and the other is still with USCIS, send USCIS a case inquiry. Ask it to consider the petitions together, and give both receipt numbers. "Family unity" is not one of USCIS's published expedite criteria, so an expedite request works only if the case meets one of them on its own (see the expedite criteria).
Petitioner: Make the link obvious

USCIS has to review everything you upload. Clear labels, ordered files and an obvious link between cases matter more than volume.

Your myUSCIS account loads case data from an internal web service. You can open that data in your browser and see fields the normal case page hides, including a status code and the times behind the last update. You only see your own case, while signed in to your own account.

Read this before you use it

This is not an official USCIS tool, and USCIS does not document it for the public. You cannot always read the codes, the data can be incomplete or out of date, and USCIS can change or remove it without notice. Use it out of curiosity only. Do not make decisions, judge your timeline or file anything based on it. For a case status you can rely on, use your myUSCIS case page and the USCIS Case Status tool.

1
Log in to your USCIS account
Go to my.uscis.gov and open My Cases, where you normally see your case status. You must stay logged in for the next step to work.
2
Open the case data in a new tab
In the same browser, open a new tab and go to https://my.uscis.gov/account/case-service/api/cases/IOE##########, replacing IOE########## with your own receipt number, for example IOE1234567890. Only your own cases will load; anything else returns an error.
3
Make the raw text readable
The page shows raw JSON, a wall of text. Look for a Pretty Print option at the top or side of the page and select it. If your browser has no such option, install a JSON formatter extension. The content stays the same; it is just easier to scan. Use your browser's find (Ctrl+F or Cmd+F) to jump to a field name.
4
Look for these fields
  • eventCode: the exact status code for the case. Codes are drawn from the NIEM benefit document status code list, though not every code there is used and USCIS does not publish its own mapping.
  • eventTimestamp: the date and time of the last case event.
  • dateUpdated: when USCIS last updated the case record in their system.
What a change here does and does not mean

A new timestamp means something changed your record. It does not mean an officer reviewed your petition: routine system updates, address changes and internal transfers all change these fields. The signals that matter are still on the case page: a move to "Actively Being Reviewed", a new document in your account, or an RFE. Processing times on myUSCIS go up and down. That means nothing.

USCIS can require any petitioner or beneficiary to attend an interview before it decides an I-130 (8 CFR 103.2(b)(9)). In a consular case it happens at a USCIS field office in the United States, while the I-130 is still pending. A petitioner living abroad must travel there.

Do not miss it. If you miss it without asking to reschedule before the appointment, USCIS treats the petition as abandoned and denies it (8 CFR 103.2(b)(13)(ii)). You cannot appeal that denial. You can only file a motion to reopen, or a new I-130 with a new fee. Call the USCIS Contact Center (1-800-375-5283) as soon as you know of a conflict.

Why it happens

USCIS does not say why it calls you. Its policy guidance lists the triggers: missing or weak documents, important inconsistencies, suspected fraud, and negative information about the beneficiary (6 USCIS-PM B.5(B); CLINIC, July 2026). A notice is not a finding of fraud.

What members report

  • Notice: usually two weeks to a month, and one member had about a week.
  • The beneficiary: the notice template asks the beneficiary to come too. The Contact Center told several petitioners with spouses abroad that only the petitioner had to attend. Call and confirm for your own case, and note the agent's name and the date.
  • The interview: short. Officers asked about the spouse (name, date of birth, hometown, job, family, past US visas) and the relationship (how you met, the proposal, the wedding, visits, children). Answer truthfully and consistently with what you filed.
  • Demand letters and writs: members reported more interviews after they started using them. Interviews happened before too, and no evidence shows either one causes an interview.

Be ready before you are asked

  • A valid US passport and photo ID, the interview notice, and a plan for travelling at short notice.
  • Originals and copies of everything filed with the I-130, including the marriage certificate, prior divorce or death certificates, and children's birth certificates and custody papers.
  • Relationship evidence dated after you filed: visits, travel records, communication and financial ties.
  • Police reports and certified court records for any arrest, even one dismissed or expunged. Certified translations of foreign documents.

Your notice has its own checklist, and that comes before this list. If the officer asks for more evidence afterwards, send exactly what they ask for, by their deadline and in their way.

If your I-130 is delayed

Think about escalating 12 months after your priority date Demand letter at 15–18 months; writ 30 days after USCIS receives the letter Reviewed 27 Sept 2026

You are here: Your I-130 has waited much longer than normal processing times, usually 12 months or more after your priority date.

What happens: You can push USCIS to act, in this order: a USCIS case inquiry, a congressional inquiry, a demand letter, then a writ of mandamus. The inquiries are requests for help. Only the writ is a legal remedy, and it can force a decision, not an approval.

Do this first: At 12 months or more, start a congressional inquiry. Speak to an AILA immigration attorney before you take any legal action.

Action list

  1. Petitioner: At 12 months or more, submit a USCIS case inquiry. Then start a congressional inquiry through one office. USCIS discusses the petition only with you or with an attorney named on a Form G-28, never with the beneficiary. You can also use the White House contact form, but it is optional and informal.
  2. Petitioner: Keep a record of every step you take. Send a demand letter at 15–18 months, once you have started and recorded the earlier steps. If USCIS has not decided 30 days after it receives the letter, the next step is a writ of mandamus.
  3. Petitioner: Before a demand letter or a writ, speak to an immigration attorney who belongs to the American Immigration Lawyers Association (AILA).
  4. Either: Read the remedies guide before you start, so you know how each step works.

Documents for this stage

  • Delayed I-130 remedies guide — each escalation step in order: case inquiry, congressional inquiry, demand letter, writ of mandamus
  • Filing a writ of mandamus yourself — how to file without a lawyer, checked against the Federal Rules

This stage is for an I-130 that has waited much longer than normal processing times: usually 12 months or more after your priority date, with no approval. You have options to push USCIS to act. This page lists them in the order you would use them. A demand letter works best at 15–18 months. A writ follows 30 days after USCIS receives a demand letter it has not answered.

Why this stage now applies to almost every consular case

Consular I-130 decisions stalled in February 2026. Since then the front of the line has barely moved past priority dates of around 25 February 2025. Anyone at or behind that date has waited 19 months or more, past both markers below, without being an unusual case.

The month markers on this page assume a moving queue. They still tell you when escalation is worth trying, but they no longer separate a delayed case from an ordinary one. See Stage 1: I-130 for the current status. At best, escalation forces a decision. It never forces an approval.

What escalation can and cannot do

Only a writ of mandamus can force USCIS to act, and it forces a decision, not an approval. Courts weigh six tests, called the TRAC factors. The law sets no number of months. A demand letter does not cause a denial: if USCIS denies the petition after one, the facts of the case caused it.

Forcing a decision can bring a problem in the file to light, such as a criminal record or a background check concern. Speak to an attorney who belongs to the American Immigration Lawyers Association (AILA) before you send a demand letter or file a writ.

Timeline
  • 12 months or more: submit a USCIS case inquiry, then a congressional inquiry through one office.
  • 15–18 months: send a demand letter, once you have recorded the earlier steps.
  • 30 days after USCIS receives the demand letter: if USCIS has not decided, file a writ of mandamus.

  • Petitioner: My petition has waited 12 months or more since its priority date, outside normal processing times.

Stage 8: Before you leave the UK

Once your visa is in your passport and your flight is booked Some tasks have deadlines Reviewed 27 Sept 2026

You are here: Your visa is in your passport and your flight is booked.

What happens: You have UK obligations to deal with before you leave. Some have deadlines that can affect your money or your rights after you go. The costliest one people miss is ISAs: you need to act on them before you become a US person for tax.

Do this first: Speak to a UK/US cross-border tax professional about any ISAs.

Action list

Tasks tagged Domicile or Public charge carry on the work from earlier stages.

  1. Beneficiary: Before you become a US person, get UK/US cross-border tax advice on any ISAs. Your choices: sell the holdings, move them into US-compliant investments, or accept complex US reporting every year.
  2. Beneficiary: Book travel that gets you to the US within 6 months of your medical exam. Your medical results last 6 months from the exam, and so does your window to enter on the visa. Check your date.
  3. Domicile, Petitioner: If you are re-establishing domicile, move before the beneficiary enters the US, or travel together. Keep proof: your US address, a job start, the first US bills.
  4. Public charge, Either: Arrange the health cover your statement described, so it starts the day the beneficiary lands.
  5. Either: File a P85 with HMRC. It tells HMRC you no longer live in the UK and sets your departure date for UK tax.
  6. Either: If you owe student loans, update your details with the Student Loans Company.
  7. Either: To keep your UK vote, register as an overseas voter before you leave.
  8. Either: Decide whether to keep paying voluntary National Insurance. You need 10 years of contributions for any UK State Pension, and 35 years for the full pension.
  9. Either: Keep your UK phone number on a cheap UK SIM or plan. UK banks, HMRC and the Student Loans Company often send security codes by text.
  10. Either: Close down your UK life: the final council tax bill, utilities, your TV licence, a Royal Mail redirect, telling your GP, and giving your banks and pension providers your US address.
  11. Beneficiary: Download your UK driving record from DVLA and ask your car insurer for a no-claims letter. Both can lower your US car insurance.
  12. Beneficiary: Ask your GP for a letter listing your prescriptions, take enough medication to last until you have a US doctor, and get copies of your medical and dental records.
  13. Either: Plan your money: keep a UK account open, decide how you will move money across, and expect larger deposits while you have no US credit history.
  14. Either: Bringing a dog? Get a CDC Dog Import Form receipt for each dog, and check your state’s and airline’s rules for every pet.
  15. Either: Moving with children? Get their school reports and records for enrolling in a US school.
  16. Either: Shipping belongings? List every item in an inventory (the Boxes tab in the master workbook), and plan to arrive in the US before your goods.

Before you leave, you have UK tasks to finish. Some have deadlines that can affect your money or your rights once you have gone. This page lists them with the costliest first.

UK tasks before you leave

Either: ISAs: act before you leave

UK movers to the US miss this more than anything else, and it costs the most. Once you become a US person (a green card holder), your ISAs lose their tax-free status for US tax. Worse, Stocks & Shares ISAs usually hold non-US funds that US tax rules treat as PFICs (Passive Foreign Investment Companies).

The US taxes PFICs heavily. You file Form 8621 for each fund every year, pay punitive tax on gains, lose the lower capital gains rates, and pay high costs to stay compliant.

If you cash out an ISA after you become a US person, PFIC tax applies. Before you become a US person, you can sell the holdings, move them into US-compliant investments, or accept the complex reporting.

Speak to a UK/US cross-border tax professional before you decide anything. Do not assume your UK accountant or a standard US tax preparer will get this right.

Either: File a P85 with HMRC

When you leave the UK, file a P85 form to show you no longer live here. The P85 stops HMRC taxing you wrongly, sets your departure date for UK tax, and may lead to a tax refund. HMRC usually mails refunds as a paper check to your US address, so make sure you have a way to deposit it.

gov.uk: P85 guidance

Either: Update your student loan details

If you still owe student loans, log in to your student loan account and tell the Student Loans Company (SLC) you are moving abroad. The SLC needs this to set your repayments, and you need it so you keep getting messages about your account.

Tell the embassy too. The consular officer weighs a UK student loan as a debt against your income. Put it in your public charge statement: the balance, the expected monthly repayment and which income pays it. Attach your latest statement. See public charge: student loans.

gov.uk: Update student loan details when moving abroad

Either: National Insurance

You can keep paying voluntary National Insurance from the US. Think it through:

  • 10 years of contributions gets you the minimum UK State Pension
  • 35 years gets you the full UK State Pension
  • Voluntary contributions cost relatively little, and the State Pension is index-linked, so it rises over time. Many UK emigrants find them good value.

gov.uk: NI contributions abroad

Either: The NHS after you move

The NHS depends on where you live, not your citizenship. Once you move to the US for good, you lose free access to most NHS care. The exceptions:

  • A&E: always free. Anyone can use A&E in the UK, wherever they live.
  • Everything else: the NHS charges overseas visitors for GP visits, prescriptions, hospital treatment and ongoing care. A consultation or treatment can cost hundreds of pounds.
  • If you move back to the UK: free access returns as soon as you live in the UK again.

Plan ahead: look at health insurance for the US. Expect NHS charges if you visit the UK for treatment while you live abroad. See NHS: Planning your healthcare abroad.

Either: Shipping your belongings

Your mover arranges US customs clearance through a customs broker. Movers ask for the same set of papers:

  • copies of your passport and immigrant visa
  • CBP Form 3299 (the declaration for unaccompanied belongings) and a supplemental declaration
  • an inventory of your belongings, in English
  • a power of attorney so the customs broker can act for you
  • the bill of lading (sea) or air waybill (air)

Your mover or customs broker supplies the current Form 3299.

Used household goods you have owned and used abroad for at least a year enter duty-free. List anything you have owned for less than a year separately, with its value and the month, year and country you bought it: it can be charged duty.

Make the inventory specific. Write what is in each box, in English. Never write “Miscellaneous” or “Contents unknown”, and describe boxes you packed yourself too. The Boxes tab in the master workbook has the columns customs and your insurer ask for.

  • Arrive before your goods. You must be in the US when your shipment arrives, or clearance is delayed and storage charges add up.
  • Sea freight: your mover files an Importer Security Filing (ISF) at least 24 hours before your container is loaded.
  • Never put your date of birth, Social Security number or passport number on a bill of lading or air waybill.
  • Insurance: your insurer asks for each item’s replacement value, which is not the same as its value for customs.
Either: Pets

Dogs need, for each dog:

  • a CDC Dog Import Form receipt. Show it to the airline before boarding and to US Customs and Border Protection on arrival. It lasts 6 months and covers repeat trips from the same country.
  • to be at least 6 months old when it arrives
  • a microchip that a universal scanner can read
  • to look healthy on arrival. Carry its medical records.

A dog that has been only in dog rabies-free or low-risk countries for the past 6 months can arrive at any US airport, seaport or land border crossing. Check the UK on the CDC’s list of high-risk countries before you book.

Cats need no proof of rabies vaccination to enter, but must look healthy. Hawaii and Guam add quarantine rules.

Every pet: check your destination state’s rules and your airline’s rules too. Either may ask for a health certificate even where the CDC does not. See CDC: Bringing an animal into the US.

Either: Keep your UK phone number

UK banks, HMRC and the Student Loans Company often confirm it is you with a code sent by text. Keep your UK number on a cheap UK SIM or plan that works abroad, so you can still log in after you move.

Either: Close down your UK life
  • Ask your council for a final council tax bill.
  • Close or move your utilities, broadband and TV licence.
  • Set up a Royal Mail redirect.
  • Tell your GP practice you are leaving.
  • Give your banks and pension providers your US address.
Beneficiary: Your UK driving record and no-claims proof

US car insurers may give credit for your UK driving history. Before you leave, download your driving record from DVLA and ask your current insurer for a no-claims letter. See your UK driving record and US car insurance.

Beneficiary: Prescriptions and medical records

Ask your GP for a letter listing your prescriptions, and take enough medication to last until you see a US doctor. Get copies of your medical and dental records while you are still registered, because the NHS charges for most care once you have moved.

Either: Credit and money

Your UK credit history does not move with you. Keep a UK account open, decide how you will move money across, and expect landlords and utilities to ask for larger deposits until you build US credit. See banking and US credit.

Either: Children’s school records

Get each child’s latest school reports and records for enrolling in a US school. Schools follow their state’s vaccination rules, which can differ from the immigration medical’s.

Either: Register as an overseas voter

To keep your UK vote, register before you leave: gov.uk/voting-when-living-abroad

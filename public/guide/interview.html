Stage 6: Embassy Interview

⏱ Interview 20–40 minutes; 1.5–4.5 hours in the embassy Arrive 30–45 minutes before your appointment South Pavilion, 33 Nine Elms Lane, London SW11 7US Reviewed 27 Sept 2026

Bottom line

Stage 6: Embassy interview. Your medical and document check are done and you have an interview date. By interview day most of the paperwork is done, but the officer still makes their own public charge assessment, now using a structured worksheet in every case. Answers must match your DS-260 and CEAC documents exactly. A decision or next step is typically communicated on the day. Do this first: make sure every financial document is already in CEAC, and send a public charge statement at least five working days ahead.

Action list

  1. Either: Make sure all financial evidence is already uploaded to CEAC. The officer decides the case mainly from CEAC, and documents first produced at the window are not guaranteed to be accepted there and then; if they are needed, the usual result is a 221(g).
  2. Beneficiary: Pack your passport, the sealed document packet from the document check, and the courier registration confirmation.
  3. Beneficiary: Bring originals of everything else, and a paper copy of what is in CEAC in case the embassy cannot see an upload.
  4. Beneficiary: Arrive 30–45 minutes early, go to the South Pavilion off Ponton Road, and tell staff: “I am here for an immigrant visa interview.”
  5. Beneficiary: Rehearse your answers against your DS-260. They must match it exactly. Rehearse with your folder in hand too, so you can find each document when the officer asks for it, and learn the balance of each bank and retirement account. Run several mock interviews, with your petitioner or a friend playing the officer: one member saw a large improvement from the first to the last (Chris's account).

US Embassy London: 33 Nine Elms Lane, London SW11 7US
Nearest stations: Vauxhall or Nine Elms. Common nearby hotel: Travelodge Vauxhall.

What officers assess

The USCIS approval, NVC's review and the medical do not decide your case. The consular officer decides independently whether the relationship is genuine, whether any ground of inadmissibility applies (criminal, security, misrepresentation, prior immigration violations, health), and whether you are likely to become a public charge. The officer can also send the petition back to USCIS. NVC checked that your paperwork was complete, not that your finances are sufficient, and its review does not bind the officer.

Public charge: what has changed
  • November 2025: officers began giving real weight to the five statutory factors (in the law since 1996) (age, health, family status, assets and finances, education and skills) about you, the beneficiary. A sufficient I-864 no longer ends the inquiry on its own. This rests on practitioner analysis of an unpublished cable.
  • 15 August 2026: a structured public charge worksheet was deployed to officers at every post.
  • 1 September 2026: mandatory public charge training and expanded managerial review of immigrant visa decisions took effect worldwide.
  • 10 September 2026: cable 26 STATE 88862 told all posts to complete the worksheet in every immigrant visa case.

The worksheet, training and review come from the government's own court filings in CLINIC v. Rubio (S.D.N.Y.) and Medani v. Trump (N.D. Cal.). Sources are listed on the public charge page.

What this means in the room. Expect questions about your own work, savings, health and qualifications, and about the petitioner's age, dependents and debts, not only your sponsor's income. A short interview is still possible, but prepare for a long one: see the community accounts below. Send a public charge statement at least five working days ahead, bring printed proof of every asset you name, and know your figures in US dollars. The public charge page has the template and the questions posts ask.

A decision or next step is usually communicated on the day. If your passport is kept and you are told they intend to approve your visa, that is good news. If the officer is not yet satisfied on public charge, the usual result is a 221(g) listing what to provide, not an outright refusal. Some cases are placed into administrative processing, and you will be told what is needed to resolve it.

US Embassy London: 33 Nine Elms Lane, London SW11 7US

Arrival

Arrive 30–45 minutes before your scheduled appointment. Go directly to the staff member at the front and state clearly: "I am here for an immigrant visa interview." This is still the right first move, but be aware that in practice the queue situation outside can be chaotic, particularly at early morning slots.

The outside queue: what to expect

Most members go straight in once they say they have an immigrant visa interview. In June 2026 one member was sent to the general queue until the building opened. On 21 October 2026 a member who arrived at 8:15 for a 9am interview was told to come back at 8:30, and at 8:30 the same staff member sent him to the front. If staff tell you to queue, join at once rather than hang back, and repeat "immigrant visa interview" at the window inside. If you are with someone, they can hold your place while you ask.

Read the sticker on the back of your passport

At check-in, staff put a sticker on the back of your passport with your queue number and booth number. They may hand the passport back without telling you. Read the sticker before you walk away, and check that the name and case number on it are yours.

The waiting area is the room used for the document check. Turn left out of the lifts, turn left at the end of the room, then turn left again into the waiting area: three banks of seats facing a wall of booths (route on Stage 5). "Left and then left" on its own takes you to a queue. On 20 October 2026 Chris, the spouse of member Jk27, heard only "left and then left", waited an hour in the wrong line and then in a second one, and received an email saying the interview needed to be rescheduled. Staff still saw them that day, as the last applicant in the building. If you are unsure you are in the right place, show your sticker to a member of staff and ask.

What to bring

Beneficiary: Core documents
  • Passport, valid for at least six months beyond your intended date of entry into the US (London's instruction, and the one to meet)
  • Sealed document packet (received at document check)
  • Courier registration confirmation
Beneficiary: No document check? Bring every original

Same-day document checks were canceled from late August 2026, so many applicants now reach the interview without one and without a sealed packet. London's interview pack for these cases, emailed with the interview notice, says originals must be presented at the interview and that failing to bring all required documents may result in your appointment being canceled. It lists:

  • Valid passport, valid for six months beyond your intended date of entry into the US
  • One passport photo (normally handed in at the document check)
  • Birth certificate: UK birth certificates must show at least one parent's name
  • Divorce decree or death certificate for every previous marriage, yours and your spouse's
  • Evidence of assets and liabilities: savings, property, bank statements, the affidavit of support, or a US job offer
  • ACRO police certificate for every applicant aged 16 or over, plus foreign police certificates for any country of citizenship you have lived in, any country where you lived more than 12 months after age 16, and any country where you were arrested
  • If applicable: adoption certificate, deed poll or statutory declaration (not needed for a change of name through marriage), military records
  • Certified translations of anything not in English
  • Your courier confirmation page from AIS

Rescheduling only goes one way. The pack says you can cancel or reschedule through AIS, but only to a date after your assigned appointment. Before you move a short-notice slot, weigh that you cannot get an earlier one back. Diversity visa applicants receive a version that also covers the DV fee, payable at the interview by card or cash (US dollars or pounds, no contactless).

If you had a document check, these are often the only documents physically reviewed, because most materials were pre-verified there. Bring originals of everything else (I-864, marriage certificate, ACRO, tax documents, DS-260 confirmation) but expect they may not be requested. Officers rely primarily on what is already uploaded to CEAC.

Upload financial evidence first, and bring paper copies

Upload every financial document to CEAC before the interview. Evidence first produced at the window may not be accepted there and then, and a 221(g) to collect it adds weeks. A paper copy of something already in CEAC can still rescue a case: in July 2026 staff could not see one sponsor's uploaded tax transcript and attached the printed copy on the spot.

Leave laptops and large bags behind.

Bag storage near the embassy

If your hotel cannot hold items, two options nearby: District Coffee (Nine Elms) and World Heartbeat Cafe.

Beneficiary: South Pavilion: where beneficiaries go for interview

The interview takes place at the South Pavilion, which is a separate building alongside Ponton Road on the south side of the embassy complex. Do not go to the main Nine Elms Lane entrance. Follow signs or ask staff to direct you to the South Pavilion Consular Services entrance.

South Pavilion is on the south side of the embassy complex, accessed via Ponton Road

Interview structure

1
Document window
Submit passport and sealed packet. Confirm courier details. Provide fingerprints if requested. Your passport comes back with a sticker showing your queue number and booth: watch for that number (see Arrival).
2
Interview window
Take oath. Answer questions. Expect 20–40 minutes: since the public charge worksheet was deployed to consular officers, the financial questioning is a sustained line of enquiry rather than a box-tick. Receive decision. Total time in embassy: 1.5–4.5 hours. Before the public charge worksheet, community members reported 30 minutes to 2 hours; the longer financial questioning has moved both ends of that.

Common interview questions

All 76 questions in the community question bank, grouped by topic: almost all reported by members interviewed at London, plus a few taken from a written public charge questionnaire and an interview at other posts. To rehearse them as flashcards or filter by visa category, use the interview question bank.

Answers must match your DS-260 and CEAC documents exactly. The interview is not a formality: the officer independently decides whether you are eligible, and inconsistencies are one of the things they look for.

Community interview experiences

All reported outcomes below are approvals. The five newest accounts come first, because they are the only ones since officers began completing a public charge worksheet in every case.

Since the public charge worksheet

Matt: IR1, 21 October 2026 (upgraded from CR1 at the document check)
A 9am appointment. Matt reached the South Pavilion entrance at 8:15 and found long lines. He asked a member of staff whether he could go to the front for an immigrant visa interview; the staff member told him to come back at 8:30. At 8:30 the same staff member sent him to the front of the line. Staff put a note on the back of his passport, and after security, reception gave him a sticker with his ticket number and sent him up the lift to floor 1, then left and left. Chris (account below) had sent him the route the day before, so he found the waiting area and sat down at 8:45. Within 10 minutes he had his document check: fingerprints and his civil documents. The officer there told him she had changed his visa from CR1 to IR1, because he and his wife had been married for more than two years.

The wait. He sat back down by 9:00 and was called at 10:40. One officer was interviewing, at about 30 minutes per applicant, and Matt was fourth. He thinks the first two, each interviewed for about 30 minutes, were approved. The applicant before him left after 5 to 10 minutes with a green slip, which he took to be a 221(g).

The interview. About 10 minutes. The officer asked about the marriage, how the couple met, who the joint sponsor is (Matt's father-in-law) and what he does for work. He then moved to public charge. He said he had read Matt's public charge statement but had to ask the questions again under oath, and most were confirmations: "So you have $x in savings?" "Yes." He asked what Matt's wife does and what Matt does, and about health insurance; Matt had bought a Cigna plan in advance. As Matt remembers it, the officer asked for four documents, and Matt handed each one over as he answered: his second passport, to check the details matched; his MSc graduation certificate, which Matt did not have yet because he finished the course the month before, so he gave the letter of enrolment; his undergraduate certificate and transcript, which the officer kept; and evidence of a world record Matt holds in robotics, which he had listed under qualifications in his public charge evidence. He asked for none of the I-864 evidence (tax returns, tax transcripts, pay stubs, W-2s), which filled most of Matt's folder, and none of the financial proof behind the public charge statement: no bank statements, savings, lease or health insurance. The officer asked about the MSc in Robotics with what Matt took as real interest. One question caught him out: "What happened in 2022, did you have secondary questioning at an airport?" It took Matt a moment to place. In 2022 he had entered the US by sailing yacht at Seattle and been paroled in, and the stamp was still in his passport. The officer accepted his explanation, approved the case, returned the civil documents and kept the passport and the transcript. Matt called it a conversation more than an interrogation, and puts the pleasant experience down to preparation.

The lessons. If staff outside tell you to come back later, come back at that time and go to the same person. Read your own passport stamps and entry history before the interview: the officer can see every US entry, and an unusual one will come up. A statement the officer has read turns the public charge questions into confirmations, but you still answer them under oath, so know your own figures. Matt's officer wanted proof of identity and qualifications, not money; Chris's officer, the day before, asked for bank and retirement statements. Bring both.
Chris: IR1, 20 October 2026 (nearly missed the call), as told by his spouse, member Jk27, the petitioner
On arrival, staff took the passport and put a sticker on the back with a queue number (113) and a booth number, without saying anything about it. The applicant put the passport away in a document folder and followed the only direction given: "left and then left". They joined the one line that fitted, waited an hour, and were then told it was the wrong line and to take another left. In the second line, the person in front explained the sticker. Staff were calling 11 and 12, so the applicant assumed there was still time. Then an email arrived: "your interview needs to be rescheduled". The applicant found a member of staff, who had them seen that day after another wait. By the end they were the last applicant in the embassy, with only the interviewer and a security guard left. Approved.

The interview. Jk27, the petitioner, is a US citizen who lived in the UK until a month before the interview and now works in the US, with their parents in New York as joint sponsors. The officer had not read the file beforehand, but she had the public charge statement and supporting documents in front of her. Her first question was what the applicant does for work. Chris works freelance, which officers find less straightforward than a salaried job, so the couple had practised presenting it in a good light and brought a document to back it up. She then asked what the petitioner does, what the joint sponsors do, and how the couple met. She asked for bank statements and retirement account statements for both of them, and the petitioner's US offer letter. All the retirement accounts are in the petitioner's name; the applicant explained that the petitioner's employers matched contributions, and the couple have been married nine years. They showed four retirement statements, a UK pension among them, from the petitioner's moves between countries. The public charge statement gave a total for savings and a total for retirement. Before the interview, the couple rehearsed on video calls, and much of that practice drilled Chris on which accounts made up each total and what each one held. Across eight different accounts, Jk27 says, remembering which bank has what is a lot. Jk27 says the practice helped Chris organise his folder so he could pull out each document as the officer asked for it. Some of that happened unprompted: she asked a question and he produced the answer from his emotional support binder. In Jk27's view, officers like it when you produce a document while you talk, and it shows you are on top of your case. Jk27 also saw a large improvement from the couple's first mock interview to their last. The applicant also handed over the petitioner's first US paystub, which arrived too late to upload to CEAC. When she asked about housing (the petitioner is in an Airbnb after the move), she was reading the public charge statement as the applicant answered, and he suggested she take a minute with it, since it sets out the plan. The applicant called the interview thorough, and had every document the officer asked for. The petitioner puts the interview at 30 minutes or more. The petitioner's verdict: "we needed EVERYTHING". Jk27's advice: include every account you hold, however small, and total them in the statement.

The lessons. Read the sticker as soon as you get your passport back, confirm with staff that you are waiting for the right booth, and if a reschedule email arrives while you are still inside, find a member of staff at once. At the window, do not count on the officer having read your file: a public charge statement that answers the housing and money questions gives you something to point to. Where the statement gives a total, bring a statement for every account behind it and learn each account's bank and balance.
K: IR1, 2026 (beneficiary already living in the US)
A UK national who has lived and worked in the US on an employment-based E-2 visa since 2017, with a US job, a mortgage and health insurance. Questions: when the applicant first lived in the US, how the couple met, whether either of them had married before, whether they have children, and whether the applicant has lived anywhere other than the US and UK. Then the public charge questions, each with a request for proof: health insurance (the card from the applicant’s wallet), whether the applicant owns or rents (deed and latest mortgage statement), current savings (a bank statement), other investments or assets (a printout of 401(k) accounts), whether either of them has used public benefits in the UK or US, highest level of education, and whether the applicant will keep the current US job. Interview duration: about 15 minutes; the applicant before took 30–35. Approved. The applicant’s view: ten years of US income, home ownership and insurance meant every public charge question had a document behind it. If you live outside the US, expect the same questions with your answers resting on plans rather than records, so bring paper for each one.
Ilma: IR1, 2 October 2026 (re-interview after a pause refusal)
A Bangladeshi national living in England, refused under the 75-country pause in May 2026 and called back for a new interview. The petitioner had moved to England in July 2026, kept her US house, and added a joint sponsor. Had submitted a public charge statement and questionnaire, the only documents the officer kept. Questions: his UK job, pay, savings and assets, whether he has used UK public benefits, his education, his US job plan and health insurance; her savings, old US job and pay, and current UK job and pay; when she bought the US house, its address, how many rooms it has and who will live there; whether her family has used US public benefits; how they know the joint sponsor, where he works, and whether he has sponsored anyone else. Approved; the officer kept his passport.

What they brought. For the petitioner: statements for three savings accounts and one retirement account, the US house deed, 2024 and 2025 tax returns, a UK employment letter and payslip, her UK visa and her degree. For the beneficiary: a savings statement, a notarized valuation of land he owns in Bangladesh, a CV, his diplomas, his UK employment contract, payslips and 2024 and 2025 tax returns. Also the civil documents, UK and Bangladesh police certificates, the I-130 approval, the DS-260 confirmation, evidence of her UK trips while they lived apart, and the joint sponsor's 2024 and 2025 tax returns. The officer kept only the public charge statement and questionnaire.

Read this with care. A joint sponsor cannot stand in for the petitioner's US domicile (petitioner domicile). This petitioner had moved only three months earlier, kept a US house and brought the deed. One approval does not show how London will treat every petitioner who has moved abroad.
Esther: spouse visa, 2026 (after the public charge change)
Had submitted a public charge statement in advance. The officer had read it and the medical report, and consulted a supervisor throughout. Questions: why the sponsor’s 2025 income was slightly below the minimum (he had opened a new business) and what he would do if it failed, whether he is in a union and has a mortgage, who she works for and whether her employer would permit her to work from the US, her savings balance and proof of the property she owns (asked for at the window), cash income from a family business, and whether she knew what insulin costs in the US. Interview duration: 35–40 minutes. Approved. Read the full account on the public charge page →

Before August 2026

These seven pre-date the worksheet. They are still accurate on the queue, the building and the two-step process, but not on length: expect substantially longer now (see the timing note in Interview structure).

Titus: CR1, July 2026
Easy security check, checked in, received a queue number and waited. Only the sealed folder and passport were physically checked: everything else was already in CEAC. One hiccup: staff couldn't see the sponsor's 2025 IRS tax transcript on their side, but the paper copy brought along was taken at document check and attached to the case there and then. Questions after the oath: how and where did you meet, where do you both work, any kids, any previous marriages, where does your spouse live. Interview duration: 2 minutes. Approved. Their advice: "Don't be stressed if you have all the documents and just be yourself."
Keely: CR1, January 2026
Skipped queue, submitted sealed packet and passport within minutes. Waited ~1 hour. Questions: where does your husband live, how did you meet, what does he do for work. Interview duration: ~40 seconds. Approved.
Walsh: CR1 + IR2 (child), January 2026
Large queue outside, bypassed immediately on stating immigrant visa. First call to submit sealed packet and passports, interview ~30 minutes later. Total time: ~45 minutes. CR1 questions covered sponsorship, how they met, wedding, travel, job plans, and custody documents. IR2 child asked about name change, prior meetings, feelings about moving. Both approved.
Neri: IR1, March 2026
Bypassed queue on arrival. Note: initial ticket had an error and required two returns to reception before the correct ticket (name and case number) was issued. Check this carefully. Documents taken at ~9:10, interview called just before 9:30. Questions: where did you meet, how many times visited the US, joint sponsor details, husband's job and location. Total time: ~2 hours. Approved.
Queen B: IR1, July 2025
Arrived 7:40 for 8:00 appointment. Sent directly to window, sticker on passport, bypassed queue. Questions: how did you meet, prior marriages, residence history. Interview ~5 minutes. Total time inside: ~30 minutes. Approved: issued same day.
HT3: IR5 (parent of US citizen), December 2025
Did not join queue. Submitted sealed folder and passport. Questions: how did your son become a citizen, relationship to joint sponsor, light background discussion. Interview ~5 minutes. Total time: ~1.5 hours. Approved.
Crimsonak: CR1, April 2026
Arrived 9:55. Waited 40 minutes for document handover, then over 2 hours for the interview. Questions: where does your wife live, what does she do, where will you work in the US, when did you meet, any prior petition. Interview duration: under 2 minutes. Approved.

Community-reported outcomes before the worksheet (self-selected, not official): about 95% approved on the spot, 3 to 4% 221(g), 1% refused. The State Department does not publish London refusal rates in this form.

Common pattern across these seven: skip the main queue, state "immigrant visa," two-step process (document window then interview window), interview 1–10 minutes, decision immediate. Check your sticker has the correct name and case number.

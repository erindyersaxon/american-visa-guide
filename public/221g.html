Section 221(g): how to prevent it, and how to resolve it

A 221(g) is a temporary refusal at the window: the officer needs something before deciding your case. At London it is most often a missing financial document, which you can supply before the interview. This page shows you the green refusal sheet and what to do at each stage to avoid one. If you already hold one, it tells you how to respond.

The short version Upload the IRS tax transcript for every sponsor before the interview. Line up a joint sponsor if income sits anywhere near the line. Make sure the petitioner can show a US domicile. Put every document the officer could ask for into CEAC beforehand. Those four steps prevent the common London financial 221(g).

What a 221(g) is

Section 221(g) of the Immigration and Nationality Act lets a consular officer refuse a visa while the application is incomplete or needs more review. It is not a final decision on your case. It is a temporary refusal. In most London cases it means the officer needs something that is not yet on file. Supply it, and the case resumes.

At London the officer hands you a green Consular Section: Immigrant Visa Unit sheet, with boxes ticked. The address to send documents to, LNDIVSubmissions@state.gov, is printed at the top. The ticked boxes are the whole of what you must clear. The sheet divides 221(g) into two types, and the type decides what happens next:

Type 1 Additional administrative processing

A mandatory background or eligibility review. The sheet says nobody can speed it up or waive it, and it has no set timeframe. You cannot check its status; the Embassy contacts you when it finishes. You often have nothing to submit, though some posts ask for your personal history and CV: see administrative processing. It is the less common type, and you control less of it.

Type 2 Submission of additional evidence

The officer needs the ticked documents before processing can continue. This is the common London 221(g), it most often concerns money, and this page aims to help you avoid it. You control the outcome: send what is ticked, and the case moves again.

A 221(g) is not a final refusal The officer has not refused your case on the merits. Most cases resolve once you supply the requested item. Each one still adds weeks to months of delay, which is why prevention repays the effort.

The most common cause: money

Community reports show most London 221(g)s concern money. Two patterns account for most of them, and you can avoid both before interview day.

1. The missing IRS tax transcript NVC accepts either an IRS tax transcript or a complete copy of the filed return. London does not always agree at the interview. Officers have issued 221(g)s for a missing transcript after NVC granted DQ, with the full return, W-2 and pay stubs on file. This applies to each financial sponsor on the case, joint sponsors included. DQ on a return copy is not the finish line. The interview is.
2. Income judged too low, with no joint sponsor ready London officers doubt cases that rest on income near the 125% poverty-guideline threshold, or on assets alone. Officers have judged income “too low” at the window and asked for a joint sponsor where the figures cleared the guideline on paper. Cases that rely on savings, property equity or investments have drawn a 221(g) despite meeting the asset test. If your margin is thin, or you lean on assets, have a joint sponsor ready.

The rule underneath both: where NVC's minimum and London's practice differ, meet London's standard before you submit. Reaching DQ on lesser evidence and then drawing a 221(g) for the same document costs far more time.

The petitioner's domicile problem

A joint sponsor can fix income. Nobody can fix the petitioner's domicile. If the petitioner cannot show their principal residence is in the United States, the petitioner cannot act as a sponsor. The officer cannot accept a joint sponsor either, and must refuse the case (9 FAM 601.14-7). It catches couples living together in the UK more than most.

  • Check for the employment exception first. Some petitioners abroad for a set period already count as US-domiciled: those who work for the US government, a recognised US research institution, or a US firm in foreign trade (or its subsidiary).
  • If your domicile has lapsed, re-establish it before the person you sponsor immigrates. You may travel together. You may not follow later.
  • A lease alone proves little. Officers look for evidence of living there: US tax filings with domestic earned income, a W-2 or US job offer, utility bills, a state ID.

Prevent it: what to do at each stage

Most 221(g)s trace back to a gap you could have seen earlier. Close these at each stage, and the officer has nothing left to request at the window.

Stage 1 · I-130 Plan; nothing to submit yet
  • If the US citizen petitioner lives overseas, plan how they will show a US domicile, up to moving back, job hunting and relocation. It needs to be in place by the time the petition reaches NVC. Read petitioner domicile now: a joint sponsor cannot cover it, and a petitioner abroad for years has the most ground to make up.
  • Ask whether you may need a joint sponsor. You may if the petitioner's income is low, they are a student, they earn no W-2 or self-employment income, or they live overseas with you.
Stage 2 · NVC Meet London's bar, not the DQ minimum
  • Upload the IRS tax transcript for each sponsor and joint sponsor, from irs.gov/individuals/get-transcript, even if NVC granted DQ on a 1040 copy. No other step prevents more 221(g)s.
  • If the transcript is not ready, upload the complete signed 1040, W-2s, pay stubs and the IRS e-file acceptance. Add a CEAC note that the transcript will follow before the interview.
  • If income is marginal, provide a full joint sponsor package: their own I-864, transcript, proof of US status and proof of US domicile.
  • Ask each sponsor and I-864A household member to check for a credit freeze before signing, and to lift it when asked. The 08/24/26 edition authorizes a credit check on each of them.
  • Upload domicile evidence for the petitioner even if NVC does not ask for it, and do not rely on a lease. Use US tax filings showing income earned in the US, a W-2 or US job offer with a start date, utility bills, a state ID. If the petitioner lives abroad, add evidence that they are re-establishing domicile.

More in the NVC stage of the guide and the NVC checklist.

Stage 3 · Waiting for your interview letter Refresh everything before interview day
  • If a tax-filing deadline has passed since DQ, upload the newest year's transcript to CEAC so the officer sees current figures.
  • Check that your passport is valid for at least six months beyond your intended entry date, and renew it now if not.
  • Get your ACRO police certificate (the Embassy accepts it for 12 months) and make sure you can reach your civil document originals.
  • Send a public charge statement with its evidence. The embassy calls it voluntary; treat it as mandatory. The statement builder writes it for you.
Stage 5 · Medical & document check Do not count on document check to catch your gaps

Embassy staff check your documents on the day of your medical, about two weeks before the interview. They sometimes flag a missing document, but they miss things.

  • Members have passed the document check with real gaps nobody flagged, such as a missing IRS transcript, missing domicile evidence or a joint sponsor they needed. They then got a 221(g) for the same item at the interview. Had staff flagged them at document check, the couple could have fixed each gap in the two weeks before the interview.
  • Treat document check as a backstop. Run your own check against the boxes on the green sheet, and arrive interview-ready whether or not staff flag anything.
Stage 6 · Interview day Everything already in CEAC
  • Get each financial document into CEAC before interview day. No rule bars evidence at the window. Officers decide from what is in CEAC, though. A document you produce for the first time in the room can draw a 221(g) asking you to send it the proper way. Bring originals, but do not rely on handing them over.
  • One exception: a paper copy of something you already uploaded to CEAC can rescue a case when staff cannot see the upload. This has saved cases at document check.
  • Make your answers match your DS-260 and CEAC. Officers probe inconsistencies.

If you already have a 221(g)

Work the ticked boxes, and only the ticked boxes.

  1. Read what is ticked. That is the whole of what you must clear. Extra material slows review. The green sheet triage turns your ticked boxes into a to-do list.
  2. Wait for the follow-up email. Within one or two days of the interview, the post emails submission instructions to the address you registered with the courier service.
  3. Submit by the route the email gives, whether an upload link, a mailing address or something else, and by no other.
  4. Send everything at once. If the officer asked for several documents, wait until you have them all. Use the email template.
  5. Act within a year. The sheet warns that if you do not act within one year of the date on the letter, the post cancels the application under INA §203(g). Submit as soon as your documents are ready.
Do not fall back on CEAC, and post only what the email asks for

Unless the email tells you to use CEAC, do not upload your response there. Members who uploaded 221(g) evidence to CEAC after a London interview, without being told to, report the documents sitting at Submitted for weeks with the case stalled.

Do not generate a courier QR code in AIS unless the email asks for physical documents. One applicant believes that doing so unprompted led the post to require everything by post.

What happens next

Your passport. If you tell the officer you can return the documents within days, they may keep your passport, which shortens the turnaround. Promise a fast return only if you can deliver it. If the officer gave your passport back, expect a request to send it once your documents are in. In one 2025 London case that request came the same day as the upload.

A mailing confirmation. Where the email asks for documents by post, GDIT, which runs the visa appointment service, emails a confirmation once the courier's service center receives them. One applicant's read, in part: “The documents you delivered to the service center at Mail Boxes Etc. Holborn have been received and will be forwarded to the U.S. Consular Section for processing.”

One London 221(g), start to finish (2025)
  • 14 November: interview, 221(g)
  • 17 November: follow-up email with submission instructions
  • 18 November: documents uploaded through the link in the email; a request for the passport arrives the same day
  • 20 November: case updated; the embassy receives the passport
  • 12 December: visa issued
  • 15 December: courier tracking email
  • 17 December: passport delivered
  • 18 December: flight to the US

About three and a half weeks from documents to visa, and five from interview to arrival. One case, not a promise.

If administrative processing is ticked instead of a document list, you often have nothing to submit. No one can expedite or waive it, and it has no published timeframe; the Embassy contacts you when it finishes. Add LNDIVSubmissions@state.gov and the courier's senders to your safe-senders list. If the post asks for your personal history and CV, see administrative processing.

If the reason you were given is vague. Cable 26 STATE 88862 (10 September 2026) tells officers who issue a new 221(g) to note the specific reasons and to spell out any reason, in the case notes and to the applicant. The instruction targets cases reworked after the vacated immigrant visa and diversity visa pauses, so it does not bind every 221(g). It still shows the standard the Department expects of its officers: if your stated reason is vague, say so when you follow up with the post. The cable is public as ECF 95-1 in Medani v. Trump, No. 5:26-cv-06332 (N.D. Cal.).

Called back to interview

If you received a 221(g) before the September 2026 interview cancellations and now have a new interview date, read re-interview after a 221(g). In short:

  • An ordinary 221(g) does not lock in the old standard. The officer decides public charge at the new interview, with the new worksheet. The officer can look at all five factors about you, not just the box ticked on your old sheet.
  • A pause-only 221(g) went back a step, not to the start, but the officer may still ask public charge questions.
  • The officer assesses anything that has changed, for both kinds. Above all, check whether the petitioner has moved out of the US, which a joint sponsor cannot fix. Check your medical too: results last 6 months, and you must enter the US within that time.

Administrative processing and technology checks

Some 221(g)s tick administrative processing and then ask for 15 years of travel, addresses and employment, plus a CV. One report from another post, in September 2026, followed an interview with detailed questions about the beneficiary's cybersecurity degree. The form matches the Department's DS-5535 security questionnaire, and the request points to a security or technology-transfer review, not a public charge finding.

Some fields have military as well as civilian uses, called dual-use: chemistry, microbiology, cybersecurity, aerospace, drones, remote sensing, AI and advanced materials. If you studied or work in one, the officer may send your case to Washington for a technology check before deciding it. Officers listen for key words from the Technology Alert List. Prepare a precise CV, a publications list and an employer letter, and describe your work in plain, accurate terms.

221(g) vs 212(a)(4) vs administrative processing vs visa refused

The State Department calls every outcome other than an issued visa a refusal, including a 221(g). What matters is the section of law the officer names, because it decides what you do next. Read the section on your sheet, then find it below. This page uses “visa refused” for a refusal that new documents cannot fix.

WhatWhat it meansWhat you do
221(g) refusal Your case is incomplete or needs more review. The officer has not decided whether you qualify. The green sheet lists what is missing. Send the ticked items. Within one year, the post reconsiders the case without a new fee (22 CFR 42.81(e)). See if you already have a 221(g).
212(a)(4) refusal The officer decided you are likely to become a public charge. This is a finding that you are ineligible, made under the public charge ground of the INA. It is a decision on the merits. No waiver exists. Send new evidence that answers the officer’s concern, such as a joint sponsor, more income or assets, a US job offer or health insurance. Upload it to CEAC and email it to the post. The letter rarely says what fell short; London’s 212(a)(4) form gives no reason at all. Answer every factor. Within one year, the post reconsiders the case. See public charge: if the officer refuses under 212(a)(4).
Administrative processing A background or security review. At the window it appears as a 221(g) with the administrative processing box ticked. After a successful interview, CEAC can also show “Administrative Processing” for a few days while the post prints the visa. That is normal. It is not a refusal. Usually nothing. Wait for the post to contact you. Some posts ask for 15 years of personal history and a CV; see administrative processing.
Visa refused A refusal that new documents cannot fix. Examples: a permanent ground such as misrepresentation under INA 212(a)(6)(C)(i), or the post sending the petition back to USCIS with a recommendation to revoke it (22 CFR 42.43). Speak to an immigration attorney. Some grounds allow a waiver on Form I-601, which USCIS decides. A petition sent back to USCIS stays there until USCIS reaffirms or revokes it.
Visa pause A 2026 policy that refused visas because of the applicant’s nationality. A court struck it down on 21 August 2026. Wait for the embassy to email you a new interview. See the visa pause, struck down. The separate travel ban, Proclamation 10998, still applies to 39 countries.

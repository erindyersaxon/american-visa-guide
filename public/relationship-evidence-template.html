Relationship evidence template

Lay out your photos and message logs so an officer can read each one in seconds: who is in it, when, where, and what it shows. Choose a few strong items from across your whole relationship. A labelled page of twelve photos says more than an unlabelled album of two hundred.

What the officer looks for

The US Embassy in Santo Domingo lists helpful proof of relationship as wedding photos, "photos of the couple's life together throughout the duration of the relationship, messages or call logs, past and recent social media posts", joint contracts or invoices, remittance receipts and joint bank statements. The consular officer who adjudicates the visa reviews it.

USCIS lists six kinds of evidence that a marriage is bona fide: joint property, a joint lease, shared finances, birth certificates of your children, affidavits from people who know you, and "any other documentation relevant to establishing that the parties did not enter the marriage for the purpose of evading immigration law". Photos and messages fall in that last group. They support the first five. They do not replace them.

USCIS judges your good faith intent "to establish a lasting relationship at the inception of the marriage". Include photos and messages from before and around the wedding, not only recent ones.

Leave these out
  • Intimate photos: in bed together, not fully clothed, or intimate displays of affection. The Santo Domingo embassy calls them "neither appropriate nor convincing" and warns they "may even be detrimental to your credibility".
  • Near-duplicates. Pick the best photo from each day or event.
  • Message screenshots that show only greetings or emoji. Choose messages about plans, visits, family, money or the visa process.
  • Anything you cannot explain at interview. Expect the officer to ask about any item in the pack.

How to fill it in
  • Cover the whole relationship: how you met, early visits, the engagement, the wedding, and visits since you filed. The months before the wedding show your intent when you married.
  • Use one row per event, with one to three photos. Name everyone in the photo, and give their relationship to you.
  • Match dates and places to your passport stamps, boarding passes and DS-260 travel history.
  • Use "Sort by date" before you print. Officers read a timeline faster than a pile.
  • Your photos and text stay in this browser. Nothing uploads. Large photos may not save between visits, so print or save as PDF when you finish.

Affidavits

Third-party affidavits Optional

A friend or relative who knows you both writes a statement about your relationship and signs it in front of a notary public. USCIS accepts affidavits "when primary or secondary evidence does not exist or is unavailable". Treat them as support for your documents. Skip this section if your joint documents and photos already cover the relationship.

Who can write one. Get at least two, from different people. The regulation calls for "two or more affidavits, sworn to or affirmed by persons who are not parties to the petition who have direct personal knowledge of the event and circumstances." Each writer must:

  • Not be a party to the petition. The petitioner and the beneficiary cannot write one.
  • Have direct personal knowledge: they saw the events themselves.

Relatives can write one, and the writer does not need to be a US citizen.

What each one contains. USCIS lists:

  • Full name, address and contact details.
  • Date and place of birth, and relationship to you.
  • A copy of their government-issued ID, if available. Attach it behind the affidavit.
  • Full details of the facts, and how they know them first-hand.

USCIS states that "affidavits that cannot be verified carry no weight". Use a phone number and email the writer answers, and make sure every date matches your photos and travel records.

Choose people who have seen you together in person: at the wedding, on visits, at family events. Ask each one to name dates and places. "They are a lovely couple" tells the officer nothing. "I hosted them for dinner in Manchester on 22 December 2024" does. The writer fills in their own details. Do not write it for them.

Get it notarized. The writer prints the affidavit unsigned, takes photo ID to a notary public, swears or affirms that it is true, and signs in front of the notary. The notary completes the block at the end and adds their seal. Keep the original. USCIS "may, at any time, request submission of an original document", and a missed deadline can lead to denial. Bring the original to your interview as well as a copy. If the writer uses a language other than English, attach a certified English translation.

  • In the United States, use a notary public commissioned in the writer's state.
  • In the United Kingdom, find a notary through the Notaries Society. UK notaries charge a fee, so ask the price when you book.

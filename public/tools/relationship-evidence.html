Relationship evidence template

Lead with records made at the time: the birth certificate, then money transfers, school records and insurance naming the child. Then lay out photos and messages so an officer can read each one in seconds: who is in it, when, where, and what it shows.

Lay out the civil records that link you to your brother or sister: your US citizenship, both full birth certificates, and the marriage, divorce and name change records that connect them. Each one proves a single fact, so the officer can check the chain in minutes.

Lead with your financial and legal records: a lease, a joint bank account, a mortgage, and money transfers with what each one paid for. Then lay out your photos and message logs so an officer can read each one in seconds: who is in it, when, where, and what it shows. Choose a few strong items from across your whole relationship. A labelled page of twelve photos says more than an unlabelled album of two hundred.

When a parent and child need this

Most parent and child petitions rest on civil records alone: the birth certificate, and the parents’ marriage certificate when the relationship runs through the father. See the I-130 checklist for your category.

A father whose child was born to unmarried parents must also show a bona fide parent-child relationship, formed while the child was unmarried and under 21, unless the child was legitimated before 18 (8 CFR 204.2(d)(2)(iii)). The same applies when an adult files for a father (IR5), or siblings who share a father (F4). The regulation asks for evidence of the father’s “active concern for the child’s support, instruction, and general welfare”, and that father and child lived together or that he held the child out as his own.

A stepparent proves the step relationship with the marriage instead: a marriage certificate dated before the child turned 18, the child’s birth certificate naming the parent, and records ending both spouses’ earlier marriages (8 CFR 204.2(d)(2)(iv)). See the stepchild example.

An adoptive parent sends the adoption decree from before the child turned 16, proof of 2 years of legal custody from a court or government order, and records showing 2 years of living together (8 CFR 204.2(d)(2)(vii)). See the adopted child example.

USCIS calls documents made at the time the most persuasive evidence. It names money order receipts or canceled checks, the father’s tax returns, medical or insurance records listing the child as a dependent, school records, letters between them, and affidavits from friends, neighbors and school officials. Photos and messages support these; they do not replace them.

What the officer looks for

A sibling petition proves a chain: the petitioner is a US citizen aged 21 or older, and the petitioner and the beneficiary share at least one parent (8 CFR 204.2(g)(1)–(2)). Civil records prove each link. Photos and messages do not.

  • You share a mother: both full birth certificates naming her.
  • You share a father: both full birth certificates naming him, his marriage certificate to each mother, and the records ending any earlier marriage of either parent.
  • Either of you was born to unmarried parents, and you share a father: that sibling also needs proof of legitimation before 18, or of a real relationship with the father before 21.
  • Any name differs between records: the marriage certificate, deed poll or court order that explains it (8 CFR 204.2(g)(5)).

If a certificate does not exist, send a letter from the registry saying so, then secondary evidence such as a religious, school or census record. Use sworn statements from people who knew the family only when none of those exist.

What the officer looks for

USCIS lists six kinds of evidence that a marriage is bona fide: joint property, a joint lease, shared finances, birth certificates of your children, affidavits from people who know you, and "any other documentation relevant to establishing that the parties did not enter the marriage for the purpose of evading immigration law". Photos and messages fall in that last group. They support the first five, but they do not replace them.

USCIS weighs evidence by where it came from. A record issued by a bank, landlord, insurer, money transfer service or registrar carries more weight than a photo, a screenshot or a statement written for the petition, because you did not create it. So each record counts on its own, with or without a photo of the same day. List every one in Financial and legal records below.

Money transfers show support, not shared finances. A transfer statement proves money moved from one of you to the other. It does not show what the money paid for, and it is not commingling: a joint account, shared bills or a jointly owned asset. In an RFE a member shared, the officer called out both gaps: payments with "no explanation or evidence of where the money was used," and "no evidence of comingled finances." If you list transfers, state what each one paid for and attach the proof, such as a rent receipt or the recipient's bank statement. Add a joint account or shared bill if you can. See how to explain transfers.

USCIS judges your good faith intent "to establish a lasting relationship at the inception of the marriage". Include photos and messages from before and around the wedding, as well as more recent ones. Don't just catalog your wedding as one event: you need to establish an ongoing relationship.

For example, the US Embassy in Santo Domingo lists helpful proof of relationship as wedding photos, "photos of the couple's life together throughout the duration of the relationship, messages or call logs, past and recent social media posts", joint contracts or invoices, remittance receipts and joint bank statements. The consular officer who adjudicates the visa reviews it.

Leave these out
  • Intimate photos: in bed together, not fully clothed, or intimate displays of affection. The Santo Domingo embassy calls them "neither appropriate nor convincing" and warns they "may even be detrimental to your credibility".
  • Near-duplicates. Pick the best photo from each day or event.
  • Message screenshots that show only greetings or emoji. Choose messages about plans, visits, family, money or the visa process.
  • Anything you cannot explain at interview. Expect the officer to ask about any item in the pack.

Tell it as a story

The officer should be able to follow your relationship from the day you met to today in a few minutes. Put your evidence in chapters, in date order. Then back up each chapter with two or three kinds of evidence from the same dates. A photo, a ticket and a message from the same night each confirm the others.

The officer should be able to see, in a few minutes, that the parent has supported, taught and cared for the child over the years. Put your evidence in chapters by what it shows, in date order within each. Back up each chapter with two or three kinds of evidence.

Affidavits

Third-party affidavits Optional

Someone who has seen the parent and child together, such as the other parent, a relative, a neighbor or a teacher, writes a statement and signs it in front of a notary public. The regulation names affidavits from “friends, neighbors, school officials, or other associates knowledgeable about the relationship” as evidence of a bona fide parent-child relationship (8 CFR 204.2(d)(2)(iii)). They carry less weight than records made at the time, so use them to cover what your documents cannot.

For a sibling petition, use affidavits only when a birth or marriage record does not exist and no religious, school or census record covers it. Get two or more, from people who were alive at the time and know the facts first-hand, such as an older relative. Each must give full details of the event and how the writer knows it, and end with a declaration under penalty of perjury. The template adds that line for you (I-130 Instructions, “What if an official document is not available?”).

A friend or relative who knows you both writes a statement about your relationship and signs it in front of a notary public. USCIS lists affidavits from people who know you as one kind of proof that a marriage is real, so you can send them alongside your records. They carry less weight than your records: the Policy Manual calls affidavits "generally considered tertiary evidence", which puts them third, behind official records and other documents (1 USCIS-PM E.6(B); see how USCIS ranks your evidence). Use them to cover a chapter your documents cannot, such as how you met. Skip this section if your financial and legal records and photos already cover the relationship.

Who can write one. Get at least two, from different people. The regulation calls for "two or more affidavits, sworn to or affirmed by persons who are not parties to the petition who have direct personal knowledge of the event and circumstances." Each writer must:

  • Not be a party to the petition. The petitioner and the beneficiary cannot write one.
  • Have direct personal knowledge: they saw the events themselves.

Relatives can write one, and the writer does not need to be a US citizen.

What each one contains. USCIS lists:

  • Full name, address and contact details.
  • Date and place of birth, and relationship to you.
  • A copy of their government-issued ID, if available. Attach it behind the affidavit.
  • Full details of the facts, and how they know them first-hand.

USCIS states that "affidavits that cannot be verified carry no weight". Use a phone number and email the writer answers, and make sure every date matches your photos and travel records.

Choose people who have seen you together in person: at the wedding, on visits, at family events. Ask each one to name dates and places. "They are a lovely couple" tells the officer nothing. "I hosted them for dinner in Manchester on 22 December 2024" does. The writer fills in their own details. Do not write it for them.

Point each statement at a record that confirms it, and list that record beside the affidavit. Spouse RFEs that members have shared say affidavits "will only be given minimal weight unless they are supported by other independent and objective evidence."

Get it notarized. The writer prints the affidavit unsigned, takes photo ID to a notary public, swears or affirms that it is true, and signs in front of the notary. The notary completes the block at the end and adds their seal. Keep the original. USCIS "may, at any time, request submission of an original document", and a missed deadline can lead to denial. Bring the original to your interview as well as a copy. If the writer uses a language other than English, attach a certified English translation.

  • In the United States, use a notary public commissioned in the writer's state.
  • In the United Kingdom, find a notary through the Notaries Society. UK notaries charge a fee, so ask the price when you book.

Example · I-130, spouse of a permanent resident (F2A)

Spouse of a permanent resident example

A finished record set for a fictional couple. Arjun Mehta, a US permanent resident in Austin, files an I-130 for his wife, Emily Watson, who lives in Leeds with her daughter, Grace. Three civil records prove the marriage, three records show it is real, and Grace goes in Part 4. This page covers what changes when the petitioner is a permanent resident; for a full set of photo and message rows, see the spouse example.

What makes it work
  • The green card proves Arjun can file. A permanent resident sends a copy of the front and back of the card (I-130 Instructions, p. 6).
  • The marriage and both spouses' earlier marriages are covered: the marriage certificate, and the decree that ended Emily's first marriage (8 CFR 204.2(a)(2)). Arjun was not married before.
  • The marriage evidence is the same as for a US citizen's spouse: records made at the time, from more than one source. The chapters match the Spouse setting in the evidence template.
  • Grace needs no petition of her own while Arjun is a permanent resident. She is listed in Part 4 and shares her mother's priority date (8 CFR 204.2(a)(4)).
  • The filing also carries Emily's Form I-130A and two passport-style photos each of Arjun and Emily.
Is this your case?
  • You become a US citizen before the visa is issued: the petition becomes an immediate relative petition and keeps its date (8 CFR 204.2(i)(3)). Your spouse's children then stop being derivatives and need their own I-130s (8 CFR 204.2(a)(4)). If you married their parent before they turned 18, file for them as stepchildren; see the stepchild example.
  • A child will turn 21 before the visa is issued: that child needs a separate petition (8 CFR 204.2(a)(4)). The Child Status Protection Act can hold a child's age for some of the wait.
  • You live outside the US: plan the affidavit of support early. See domicile.
  • Check the Visa Bulletin for the F2A wait.
Did you get your green card through an earlier marriage?

If you married again within 5 years of becoming a permanent resident, USCIS cannot approve the petition unless you prove by clear and convincing evidence that the earlier marriage was genuine, or that marriage ended with your former spouse's death (8 CFR 204.2(a)(1)(i)(A)). Send records covering the earlier marriage: joint property, a joint lease, shared finances, birth certificates of children you had together, and affidavits. If the evidence falls short, USCIS denies the petition; you can file again once you have been a permanent resident for 5 years.

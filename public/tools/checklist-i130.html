Part of Stage 1: File the I-130

I-130 Pre-Submission Checklist

Work through this before you file. USCIS can deny an I-130 that is missing required evidence without sending an RFE first (Policy Manual, Vol. 1, Part E, Ch. 6.F).

0 of 44 items checked

1. Case classification

Two interviews are possible. USCIS can interview the petitioner in the US before it decides the I-130 (6 USCIS-PM B.6(D)(1)). Petitioners may be more likely to be interviewed at the I-130 stage when the petitioner filed an earlier spousal petition for someone else, or an earlier spousal petition was withdrawn or denied. Petitioners report more of these interviews. Every consular case also has a mandatory interview for the beneficiary at the embassy after NVC.

2. Forms and filing integrity

Critical: Petitioner names and signatures must be in the correct spaces on the form. Wrong placement is a documented cause for I-130 denial. Check this carefully.

Note: The I-130A must be filed for every spouse beneficiary. The beneficiary does not need to sign it if they live outside the US.

3. Document categories at I-130 stage

Category Required Notes
Civil (Identity) ✓ The petitioner proves US citizenship (US birth certificate, unexpired US passport, naturalization certificate or certificate of citizenship, or CRBA) or permanent residence (front and back of the green card). Birth certificates for both the petitioner and the beneficiary, certified copies with parents’ names.
Relationship ✓ Spouses: the marriage certificate, a certified copy from the civil registry, timely registered. A church or ceremony certificate, or a marriage license, is not enough (6 USCIS-PM B.6(C)(1)). If either spouse was married before, every divorce decree or death certificate. Children, parents and siblings: see Section 4 for the documents that prove your category.
Translation ✓ Every document not in English must come with a full English translation, certified by the translator as complete and accurate and as competent to translate (8 CFR 103.2(b)(3)). This applies to every foreign-language document, not only when USCIS asks.
Forms ✓ I-130 (completed and signed by petitioner). I-130A for every spouse beneficiary; it only needs the beneficiary's signature if they are in the US.
Bona fide evidence ✓ (spouses) Spouses also prove the marriage is real: see Section 5. A father of a child born to unmarried parents proves a real parent-child relationship instead: see Section 4.
Medical n/a Medical exam not required at I-130 stage. Completed at later stages (NVC, Medical, Interview).
Police n/a Police certificates not required at I-130 stage. Required at NVC stage: order toward the end of the I-130 wait (ACRO takes about 30 days and is valid 12 months).

Send primary evidence: the record from the civil registry, court or government that registered the event. USCIS gives church records and other secondary evidence less weight, and accepts them only with a letter from the authority saying the primary record does not exist. Affidavits come last. 4. Proof of the family relationship

Open your category and send every document it lists. Each petition proves the link between the petitioner and the beneficiary with civil records: who was born to whom, and who married whom (I-130 Instructions, pp. 6–8; 8 CFR 204.2).

Spouse: CR1/IR1 (US citizen) or F2A (permanent resident)
  • Marriage certificate, and the divorce or death record for every earlier marriage of either spouse.
  • Form I-130A, and two passport-style photos each of the petitioner and spouse.
  • Evidence the marriage is real: Section 5.
  • Clear and convincing evidence of a good-faith marriage if you married while the spouse was in removal proceedings (with a written request for the bona fide marriage exemption), or if the petitioner got a green card through an earlier marriage less than 5 years ago (I-130 Instructions, p. 6; 8 CFR 204.2(a)(1)).
  • F2A: the spouse’s unmarried children under 21 can be listed in Part 4 as derivatives. A US citizen files a separate I-130 for each child.
Child or son or daughter: IR2, F1, F2A, F2B, F3
  • Mother filing: the child’s birth certificate showing her name.
  • Father filing: the child’s birth certificate showing both parents, the parents’ marriage certificate, and records ending any earlier marriage of either parent (8 CFR 204.2(d)(2)(i)).
  • Parents not married at the birth, father filing: proof of legitimation before 18, or records made at the time showing a real parent-child relationship before the child turned 21 and while unmarried (8 CFR 204.2(d)(2)(ii)–(iii)). See the parent-child example.
  • Stepchild: the marriage certificate of the stepparent and the child’s parent, dated before the child turned 18 (8 CFR 204.2(d)(2)(iv)).
  • Adopted child: the adoption decree from before age 16, and proof of 2 years of legal custody and 2 years living together (8 CFR 204.2(d)(2)(vii)). If the child comes from a Hague Convention country, check the USCIS family-based adoption rules first.
  • F1 or F2B (unmarried, 21 or over): if the son or daughter was married before, the divorce or death record that ended it.
  • F1, F2B and F3: list the beneficiary’s spouse and children under 21 in Part 4. They can immigrate as derivatives without their own petition (I-130 Instructions, p. 1). A US citizen filing IR2 files one I-130 per child, with a copy of the spouse’s I-130 receipt if you also filed for your spouse.
Parent of a US citizen: IR5 (petitioner 21 or older)
  • Mother: the petitioner’s birth certificate showing her name.
  • Father: the petitioner’s birth certificate showing both parents, the parents’ marriage certificate, and records ending any earlier marriage of either (8 CFR 204.2(f)(2)(i)).
  • Father, parents not married at the birth: legitimation before 18, or proof of a real parent-child relationship before the petitioner turned 21 (8 CFR 204.2(f)(2)(ii)–(iii)).
  • Stepparent: the stepparent’s marriage to your parent, dated before you turned 18 (I-130 Instructions, Item H).
  • Adoptive parent: the adoption decree from before you turned 16, and proof of 2 years of custody and living together (8 CFR 204.2(f)(2)(iv)).
  • Each parent needs their own I-130. A parent’s spouse is not a derivative (8 CFR 204.2(f)(4)). Link the two petitions: see Stage 1, Linking family I-130 applications.
Brother or sister: F4 (petitioner a US citizen, 21 or older)
  • Both birth certificates, showing at least one parent in common (8 CFR 204.2(g)(2)(i)).
  • Common father: the parents’ marriage certificate and records ending any earlier marriages. Different mothers: the father’s marriage certificate to each mother (8 CFR 204.2(g)(2)(iv)).
  • Common father, parents not married at a birth: legitimation before 18, or proof of each sibling’s real relationship with the father before 21 (8 CFR 204.2(g)(2)(ii)–(iii)).
  • Through adoption or a stepparent: the adoption or step-relationship documents for each of you (I-130 Instructions, Items H and I).
  • List the sibling’s spouse and children under 21 in Part 4. They get the same priority date without their own petition (8 CFR 204.2(g)(4)).

A birth or marriage record is missing. Send a letter from the civil authority saying the record does not exist, plus secondary evidence: a religious record of a baptism within two months of birth, a letter from the first school attended, or a census record. Only if none exist, send two or more sworn statements from people alive at the time who know the facts (I-130 Instructions, p. 8; 8 CFR 204.2(d)(2)(v)). See how USCIS ranks your evidence.

DNA. If other evidence of a parent-child relationship leaves doubt, you can offer DNA results from an AABB-accredited lab. USCIS can also ask for a test. Never send samples with the petition (I-130 Instructions, p. 8).

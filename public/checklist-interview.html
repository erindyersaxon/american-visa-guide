Real interview examples

Ilma IR1 Pause re-interview 2 October 2026

The case. The beneficiary is a Bangladeshi national who has lived in England since 2022. At his first interview on 15 May 2026 the officer told him everything was in order but handed him a 221(g) because of the 75-country pause. London called him back for 2 October 2026. The petitioner, a US citizen, moved to England on a UK dependent visa in July 2026, after the case went documentarily qualified, and the couple added a joint sponsor. Result: ✓ APPROVED The officer kept his passport and gave him the visa receipt information sheet.

What the officer asked him. His job in the UK and his pay. Whether he has used public benefits in the UK. His savings and other assets. His education. His employment plan in the US, and his US health insurance plan. Then the petitioner: her savings, her old US job, where it was and what it paid, and her UK job and pay now. When she bought her US house, its address, how many rooms it has and how many people will live there. Whether she or any of her family have used public benefits in the US. On the joint sponsor: how they know him, where he works, and whether he has sponsored anyone else.

What they brought. The civil documents, UK and Bangladesh police certificates, two photos and his UK driving licence. For public charge: a public charge statement and questionnaire. For the petitioner, statements for three savings accounts and one retirement account, the US house deed, 2024 and 2025 tax returns, a UK employment letter and payslip, her UK visa and her degree. For the beneficiary, a savings statement, a notarized valuation of land he owns in Bangladesh, a CV, his school and university diplomas, his UK employment contract, payslips, and 2024 and 2025 tax returns. Also the I-130 approval, the DS-260 confirmation page, the medical papers, evidence of her trips to the UK while they lived apart, the joint sponsor's 2024 and 2025 tax returns, and photos with the joint sponsor. The officer kept only the public charge statement and questionnaire.

Read this with care. A petitioner living abroad must still show US domicile, and a joint sponsor cannot stand in for it (the domicile problem). This petitioner had moved only three months earlier, kept a US house, and brought the deed; the house questions read as the officer testing that tie. One approval does not show how London will treat every petitioner who has moved abroad. See re-interview after a 221(g).

Esther CR1 Public charge era 2026

Duration: 35–40 minutes, and would have run longer had she not had a 2pm hotel checkout. Officer: consulted a supervisor throughout. Preparation: had submitted a public charge statement in advance; the officer had read it, along with the medical, and ran a thorough background check.

What was asked. How she met her husband, what he does, and then a sustained financial enquiry: why his 2025 income was slightly below the minimum on the tax transcript (he had opened a new business), what the business is, and what he would do if it failed. Whether he is in the union, given where he works. Whether he has a mortgage. Where he lives, and who else lives on the land.

Her own work took the longest. Who she works for, when she joined, her previous employer, whether the work is remote, whether the company would permit her to work from the US, and what her plan was if not. The officer searched her employer online during the interview and read the results back to her. Also: what she studied, where, and what other certifications she holds.

Evidence was requested at the window. Her savings balance, which she showed from printed bank and investment statements. Proof of two properties she owns and co-owns with family, of which she could evidence only one in the room. Full mechanics of her cash-paid side work in her mother’s property management company. Her monthly earnings, which she gave in USD.

On health: what insurance she has, whether she is on her husband’s plan, and — she has Type 1 diabetes — whether she knew what insulin costs in the US and had looked into it.

Her own caveat: she works in a niche digital finance field, so some questioning was tailored to an unusual profile. The pattern still holds. Anything distinctive in your case — a new business, a weak tax year, a chronic condition, remote work, co-owned property, cash income — generates its own branch of questioning, and the officer follows each branch until satisfied. Full preparation guidance: the public charge page.

What officers typically ask

Most common questions (almost always asked):

  • How did you meet your spouse?
  • Where does your spouse currently live?
  • What does your spouse do for work?
  • What do you do for work?
  • Where will you live in the US?
  • Has either of you been married before?

Sometimes asked: Joint sponsor info, when you met in person, how long together, medical insurance plans, prior travel to US, children (if applicable).

Increasingly asked, and not on the list above: why a sponsor’s income was low in a given tax year and what happens if the cause recurs; what a self-employed sponsor would do if the business failed; whether your employer will permit you to work from the US and what your plan is if not; what your medication costs in the US; and proof, shown at the window, of the savings and property you have claimed. These come from the public charge assessment rather than the relationship enquiry. Prepare them from the public charge page.

Key insight: The officer is making an independent decision, not just confirming your application: they check the relationship is genuine, look for inconsistencies and assess public charge. Be honest, accurate and conversational.

Timing reality

Total time in embassy: 1.5–4.5 hours. Before the public charge worksheet, community members reported 30 minutes to 2 hours; the longer financial questioning has moved both ends of that.

  • Check-in: 10–15 minutes (if you skip the main queue)
  • Wait for document handover: 30–90 minutes
  • Document handover: 2–5 minutes
  • Wait for interview: 30–120 minutes (longest part)
  • Interview itself: 20–40 minutes

Pro tip: Bring a book or download entertainment. No phone signal inside the embassy. Bathrooms available but limited.

What community says

  • Skip the queue: Go straight to the desk at the front. Tell staff "I have an immigrant visa interview."
  • Arrive 15–30 min early: Even though you'll wait, you skip the queue faster.
  • The most important documents are your sealed folder + passport: Everything else you bring is unlikely to be checked, but good to have as insurance.
  • Remember: Anything you are relying on should already be in CEAC. Bring originals and copies as well.
  • Wear business casual: Described as putting your best self forward.
  • Be friendly and conversational: Officers described as nice and relaxed. Some even joke.
  • Don't stress: By interview stage, you've been through the hardest parts!
  • Bring original beneficiary civil documents: e.g., birth certificate, marriage certificate, police certificates, military records, court records, etc.

Approval rates & next steps

Community-reported outcomes (self-selected, not official): ~95% approved on the spot | ~3–4% 221(g) | ~1% refused. People with good outcomes are more likely to report them, and these figures pre-date the public charge worksheet. The State Department does not publish London refusal rates in this form.

If approved: Told immediately. Given domestic violence pamphlet. Passport returned in 3–7 days (usually ~5 days). Visa issued same day or next day.

4. If approved

Passport is retained for visa printing. You'll receive an email from the embassy with collection instructions.

Typical passport return: Mail Boxes Etc (MBE): 2–3 days for pickup, 5–7 days for mail delivery.

See the data tracker for current community averages on passport return times. Next: Life in the USA

5. If not approved

221(g) with documents ticked: the most common London outcome short of approval. Supply exactly what is ticked on the green sheet, all at once, after the follow-up email. You have one year from the date on the sheet. Tick your boxes and get a to-do list · How to resolve it.

221(g) with administrative processing ticked: a different thing. A mandatory review with no set timeframe that cannot be expedited; often nothing to submit, but some posts ask for 15 years of history and a CV. What it means · History worksheet.

Refused before, now called back to interview: see re-interview after a 221(g).

Visa pauses: both the nationality visa pause and the diversity visa pause have been struck down, and the State Department rescinded the guidance behind them on 10 September 2026. Cases refused solely under either are being reconsidered. See both visa pauses vacated.

Denial: Rare. Officer explains reason. Limited recourse; consult attorney.

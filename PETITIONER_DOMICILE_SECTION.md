## 1. Bottom line

1. **The site has a gap with refusal-level consequences.** Domicile appears ~12
   times across the site and is framed as a joint-sponsor concern or a checkbox
   every time. Nowhere do we say the thing that matters most: **if the
   petitioner fails domicile, a joint sponsor cannot rescue the case and the
   applicant is refused.** That is not our inference — it is the State
   Department's written instruction to consular officers.
2. **Our readers are optimistic about this in a way the rules do not support.**
   The common UK-side pattern — US citizen petitioner living in the UK with
   their partner while the case runs — is squarely the fact pattern that
   triggers the problem. Most such petitioners do not know they have one.
3. **There is good news we are not telling anyone.** A citizen abroad working
   for the US government, a US firm developing foreign trade, a recognised US
   research institution, an international organisation, or a US religious body
   is **already** treated as US-domiciled. This carve-out is absent from the
   entire site and plausibly covers a meaningful share of London petitioners.
4. **One correction to my earlier note in this file.** I previously wrote that
   the rules provided "no mechanism" for a joint sponsor to cure petitioner
   domicile, rather than an express prohibition. That was too cautious and it
   was wrong. There is an express rule. Corrected throughout.
5. **All of it is now live** (§7): the explanatory section on `/221g.html`,
   the four aligned checklists, and cross-links from the guide, the I-864
   worksheet and the public charge page.

## 3. Why this matters more than the site currently implies

**The rule.** 9 FAM 601.14-7(a)(3)(c), the Department's guidance to consular
officers, verbatim:

> If a petitioner cannot satisfy the domicile requirement, the petitioner fails
> to qualify as a "sponsor" for the purposes of submitting Form I-864. **A joint
> sponsor cannot be accepted and the applicant must be refused pursuant to
> INA 212(a)(4).**

**Why that is different in kind from every other financial problem we cover.**
Our existing financial guidance is all about *curable* gaps — a missing
transcript, income below threshold, a joint sponsor needed. Those cost time. The
petitioner's domicile is a condition of being a sponsor at all, and the
petitioner is the sponsor by definition (601.14-5(a)(3)). There is no second
filer who can supply it.

Put plainly for the site: **petitioner income can be zero and the case
survives. Petitioner domicile cannot be zero.**

**And living abroad is presumptively disqualifying**, which is the part readers
will not expect. 601.14-7(a)(2)(a): a petitioner maintaining a principal
residence outside the US "could not normally claim a U.S. domicile and would be
**ineligible to submit Form I-864**."

## 4. Where reader optimism and the rules diverge

This is the section to write the page against. Each row is a belief we can
expect our readers to hold, and what the guidance actually says.

| What people assume | What the rules say |
|---|---|
| "I'm a US citizen, so I'm US-domiciled." | Citizenship is not domicile. Domicile is your principal residence in the US plus intent to keep it (601.14-7(a)(1)(a)). |
| "I'm only in the UK temporarily, so I'm fine." | Only if you left for a "limited, and not indefinite, period." Moving to be with a partner, no end date, fails that test. |
| "A joint sponsor covers us." | A joint sponsor covers **income**. It cannot be accepted where the petitioner fails domicile. |
| "I have a US lease/mortgage, that's my proof." | Not on the Department's list of what establishes domicile. It evidences an address, not a residence. |
| "I'll move over once the visa is issued." | You must be resident, or arriving, **no later than the date the applicant immigrates**. You may travel together; you may not follow later. |
| "NVC accepted it, so we're fine." | Already covered elsewhere on the site, and it holds here too: clearing NVC is not the consular officer's decision. |

## 5. What the section has to explain

Four positions a petitioner can be in. Readers must be able to identify theirs
in about ten seconds — that is the design constraint on the copy.

| Position | Test | Reader takeaway |
|---|---|---|
| **Living in the US** | Principal residence in the US. | No issue. |
| **Abroad, US home kept** | Left for a limited not indefinite period; intended to keep US domicile on departure; convincing continuing ties. Named examples: students, contract workers, NGO volunteers. | The "not indefinite" limb is what catches people. |
| **Abroad on qualifying US employment** | INA 319(b)(1) list, see below. | **Automatically domiciled.** The good news. |
| **Abroad, domicile gone** | Must take real steps *and* take up US residence at or before the applicant's immigration. | The work case. Start at I-130. |

**The qualifying employment list** (601.14-7(b)): the US government; a US
institution of research recognised by DHS; a US firm or corporation engaged in
whole or in part in developing US foreign trade or commerce, **or a subsidiary
thereof**; a public international organisation the US belongs to by treaty or
statute; clergy of a US religious denomination; a missionary for a US religious
or interdenominational mission body. The third item is broad.

Also worth carrying: an LPR sponsor abroad is US-domiciled if they obtained the
preservation-of-residence benefit under INA 316(b) or 317 (601.14-7(a)(1)(b)).

## 6. The evidence question (decision 1)

**The finding.** The Department's list of what establishes domicile —
601.14-7(a)(3)(b) — is: opening a US bank account, transferring funds, making US
investments, seeking US employment, registering children in US schools, applying
for a Social Security number, voting. **Property, mortgages and leases are not
on it.**

The London green sheet *does* list property and leases. These are two lists
doing different jobs: the sheet asks for proof of a domicile you claim to have;
the Department's list is about steps that establish one. Where they diverge,
the divergence is informative.

**Supporting the point:** housing appears in the guidance only as an address to
be *paired* with presence — the sponsor "must establish an address (a house, an
apartment, or arrangements for accommodations with family or friend) **and**
either must have already taken up physical residence." And the officer's
standard is factual: they must be satisfied the sponsor "has, **in fact**, taken
up principal residence in the United States."

**Recommended wording line:** a lease is on the Embassy's list, include it, but
it evidences an address, not a residence — do not let it be your only domicile
evidence. Weight, not admissibility.

**Two cheap wins to surface for readers:** applying for a Social Security number
and voting are both on the Department's list, and both are available to a
citizen who has never lived in the US as an adult.

## 8. Scope note: K-1 cases are different

The I-864 domicile requirement **does not reach K-1 applicants at the interview**
(601.14-3(b)(3)(d)). They file an I-134; the I-864 and its domicile condition
arrive later, at adjustment of status.

This corrects an error in my first draft of this note, which addressed "spouse
or fiancé(e)" in one breath. The London green sheet is a combined sheet carrying
both the I-864 boxes and the K-1 intent-to-marry box, which makes the conflation
easy. The published copy must not repeat it.

K-1 petitioners still carry real domicile risk, on different footing: the public
charge assessment, the intent to marry **and reside**, and the fact that the
full requirement lands at adjustment. Deferred, not avoided. **Write the section
for IR/CR/F cases and give K-1 a short labelled carve-out** — drafted in §9.

## 9. Copy as shipped (reference)

> ### The petitioner's domicile problem
>
> A joint sponsor can fix income. Nobody can fix the petitioner's domicile.
>
> The State Department's guidance to consular officers is blunt about this. If
> the petitioner cannot satisfy the domicile requirement they "fail to qualify
> as a sponsor," and then: *"A joint sponsor cannot be accepted and the
> applicant must be refused pursuant to INA 212(a)(4)."*
>
> That is the whole problem in one sentence. Low petitioner income is
> survivable: a joint sponsor files alongside and the case goes on. A petitioner
> who is not US-domiciled is not a paperwork gap, and there is no second filer
> who can supply it.
>
> This catches UK-side couples disproportionately, because the normal thing to
> do while a case is running is to be together in the UK.
>
> **Domicile means your principal residence is in the United States and you
> intend to keep it there.** Both halves. Which leaves four positions.
>
> **You live in the US.** Nothing to solve.
>
> **You are abroad temporarily and kept your US home.** You must show you left
> for a limited, not indefinite, period; that you meant to keep your US domicile
> when you went; and that you have real continuing ties. "Limited, not
> indefinite" is what catches people: moving to be with your partner, with no
> end date, is not a temporary absence however many US accounts you hold.
>
> **You are abroad on qualifying US employment.** If you work for the US
> government, a US firm or its subsidiary engaged in developing US foreign trade,
> a recognised US research institution, a public international organisation the
> US belongs to, or as clergy or a missionary for a US religious body, you are
> *already* treated as US-domiciled while abroad. Check this before assuming you
> have a problem. It is a real carve-out and people qualify without knowing.
>
> **You are abroad and your US domicile is gone.** You are re-establishing it.
> The officer must be satisfied you have taken real steps *and* that you have
> taken up residence in the US, or will do so no later than the date the person
> you are sponsoring immigrates. You may travel together. You may not follow on
> later.
>
> **What counts.** The Department's list of what establishes domicile is:
> opening a US bank account, transferring funds, making US investments, seeking
> US employment, registering children in US schools, applying for a Social
> Security number, and voting. Notice what is not on it: property, a mortgage, a
> lease. The Embassy will accept a lease as part of a package and you should
> include one if you have it, but it evidences an address, not a residence. A
> landlord does not know whether you are in the country. What carries weight is
> evidence generated by *being there*: US tax filings showing domestic earned
> income, a W-2, a US job offer with a start date, utility bills in your name
> across several months, school enrollment. The standard the officer applies is
> whether you have "in fact taken up principal residence" in the United States.
>
> Your address can be a house, an apartment, or an arrangement to stay with
> family or friends. You do not need a lease for that part.
>
> **Citizenship is not domicile.** A US citizen who has lived abroad since
> childhood may have no adult US domicile to point back to: no US earned income
> history, no state ID, no utility record, possibly no Social Security number and
> no voting record. That is four of the seven items on the Department's own list
> unavailable from a standing start. If that is you, begin at the I-130 stage,
> not at the interview.
>
> **If you are the petitioner on a K-1,** this is not describing your paperwork.
> The I-864 is not required of K visa applicants at the interview; you file an
> I-134 instead. It is still describing your risk. A petitioner who is not in the
> United States weakens both the public charge picture and the premise that you
> will marry and live there, and the full I-864 domicile requirement arrives when
> your fiancé(e) adjusts status. Deferred, not avoided.
